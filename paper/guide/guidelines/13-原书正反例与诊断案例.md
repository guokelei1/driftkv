# Counter-examples and diagnostic cases from the original book

## Principle of use

The most valuable part of the original book is not merely telling the author "what the Introduction should contain", but showing how a very small change alters the reader's understanding of the paper. Each case below should be used in five steps:

1. Identify the minimal difference between the counter-example and the correct example.
2. Determine whether the difference changes time, ownership, evidence strength, scope of modification or paragraph function.
3. Predict how the reader will misread the original sentence.
4. Make the minimal fix without changing the scientific fact.
5. Turn the fix into a check question for the next round.

The short sentences below retain the example patterns used repeatedly in the original book; some longer examples are diagnostic versions that compress the book's principles. Locations are given by Unit and subsection.

Genre note: these sentence-level cases are genre-independent and apply unchanged to systems-conference papers. Map the section names when applying them: "Methods" cases → Design/Implementation/Evaluation-setup text; "Results" cases → Evaluation text; "Discussion" cases → Evaluation discussion subsections, Related Work and Conclusion (see guideline 14 §3).

## I. Whole-text narrative and Introduction

### Case 1: A literature list vs a research map

List-only writing:

> Smith et al. studied X. Jones et al. studied Y. Lee et al. studied Z.

Writing with narrative function:

> A pioneering study demonstrated X. Subsequent studies extended this approach to Y. However, whether the same mechanism applies to Z remains unclear.

Both groups of sentences can carry correct citations, but only the second group indicates the starting point, the development and the gap. The first group hands "literature facts" to the reader to organise, and the reader does not know whether Z is an extension of X, a conflict with X, or a completely different question.

Check questions:

- What function does each key reference perform in this paragraph?
- Is the relation between references temporal, methodological, by object, or by result?
- Does the end of the paragraph state clearly what is still unknown?

### Case 2: A current gap vs a historical gap

Writing that stays relevant to the present:

> Although the effect of A on B has been demonstrated, little attention has been paid to the role of C.

Writing that easily closes the gap in the past:

> Although the effect of A on B was demonstrated, little attention was paid to the role of C.

has been paid links the existing research to the current state of knowledge; was paid easily leads the reader to understand "attention was not paid then", without making clear whether the problem has since been solved. This is not a simple tense choice, but a judgement about the current state of the research field.

### Case 3: A past result vs current knowledge

> They found that pressure increased the rate.

More easily read as the result of a particular past study.

> They found that pressure increases the rate.

More easily read as a relation still held to be true, or as general. If the author only wants to report a single experimental result, the Past Simple is safer; if the result is to serve as still-valid background knowledge, the Present Simple is justified.

Check questions:

- Is this a single observation in a past study, or a law currently accepted?
- Do the reporting verb and the clause verb express the same knowledge time?
- Has a single result been written as a universal law without evidence?

### Case 4: A polite and defensible gap

Higher risk:

> No one has studied C.

More defensible:

> Although C has been considered in related systems, its effect under condition Z remains unclear.

"No one" requires the author to prove that no relevant study exists; the second sentence limits the gap to an object and a condition, and also acknowledges existing work. In review, absolute negations should be turned first into locatable scope deficiencies.

### Case 5: The end of the Introduction is not "more research is needed"

Lacking action:

> More research is needed.

Writing that establishes this paper's action and expectation:

> To address this gap, we examined whether C modifies the response of B to A. We used X and found that C increased the response under condition Y.

The former does not state what needs to be studied, nor does it establish the expectation of Methods and Results; the latter lets the reader know the chain between the question, the method and the main finding.

Source: Unit 1, 1.2.2, 1.5.1–1.5.4.

## II. Methods

### Case 6: A lab record vs a reproducible method

Close to a lab note:

> An Ag wire was attached to the electrode. The cathode was sealed. The sample was tested.

Reader-oriented writing:

> An Ag wire was attached to the electrode with Ag paste in order to form an electrical contact. The cathode was then sealed to prevent leakage before the sample was tested.

The second version supplies material, purpose, order and risk control. Methods is not proof that "the author did it", but a means for a competent reader to judge "whether someone else could reproduce it".

Check questions:

- Why was each key action done?
- Which action controlled leakage, contamination, drift or contact problems?
- Can the reader recover the actual sequence?

### Case 7: then/next vs a real temporal condition

Vague:

> The solution was stirred. Then the pH was measured. Next, the sample was collected.

Precise:

> The solution was stirred until stable pH readings were obtained, at which point the sample was collected.

then and next only tell the reader the order; until stable pH readings were obtained states the stopping condition, and at which point states the sampling trigger. If stability affects the data, the former is a reproduction risk.

### Case 8: A past operation vs a standard procedure

A specific operation in this experiment:

> A flexible section was inserted into the device before measurement.

A standard procedure description:

> A flexible section is inserted into the device before measurement.

The past tense indicates a single completed operation; the present tense reads more like a general operating protocol. Both may be correct; the key is not to let the reader think this study only cites a standard step, or that the author is claiming a single operation as a universal rule.

### Case 9: Whether an existing method was modified

Insufficient information:

> The assay was performed as previously described.

Clearly distinguished:

> The assay was performed as previously described, with the following modifications: the incubation time was reduced to 10 min and the buffer was replaced with X.

If the method is entirely the same, a locatable source is needed; if there are modifications, the modified items are themselves the information needed for reproduction. Without listing the modifications, the reader cannot tell whether a difference in results comes from the object of study or from a change in method.

### Case 10: Active disclosure of a minor problem

Over-assurance:

> The samples were transferred without any complications.

More credible:

> Brief contact with the surface occurred during transfer, but the resulting variation was negligible.

The second sentence does not hide a real minor problem, and at the same time states that its effect was small. A limitation in Methods is not self-denial, but helps the reader evaluate whether the error is large enough to change the conclusion.

Source: Unit 2, 2.2.2, 2.5.1–2.5.3.

## III. Results

### Case 11: A figure cannot replace a written finding

Reporting only the figure number:

> The results are shown in Figure 1.

Locating and pointing out the pattern:

> Figure 1 shows a substantial increase in response at high pressure. This increase was observed in all three samples.

The first sentence sends the reader to the figure; the second tells the reader what to look at, and whether the pattern is consistent. The main text should not merely repeat the figure caption, nor leave the reader to do the most important comparison alone.

### Case 12: The same number, different evaluation

Neutral:

> The effect occurred in 23% of cases.

Indicating a high proportion:

> The effect occurred in as many as 23% of cases.

Indicating a low proportion:

> The effect occurred in only 23% of cases.

The number itself has no automatic meaning. as many as and only give a direction of interpretation, but must be supported by a field benchmark, an expected proportion or a comparison. Do not add evaluative words at random just to make the result seem important.

### Case 13: Grading observation, correlation and causation

Strong cause:

> X caused Y.

Weaker causal contribution:

> X contributed to Y.

Correlation:

> X was associated with Y.

Cautious inference:

> Y may have resulted from X.

If the study design can only observe covariation, associated with is more accurate than caused; if there is a reasonable mechanism but other explanations have not been ruled out, may have resulted from is more appropriate. Writing correlation as causation is the most dangerous "polishing" in Results.

### Case 14: The boundary between Results and Discussion

Limited interpretation is allowable in Results:

> These results suggest that the treatment may alter the response.

Over-stepping when evidence is insufficient:

> These results prove that the treatment changes the response by mechanism X.

The former preserves the distance between observation and interpretation; the latter simultaneously claims fact, cause and a specific mechanism. If the mechanism has not been directly tested by experiment, it should be raised in the Discussion as a constrained interpretation, not written in the Results as a proof.

Source: Unit 3, 3.2.2, 3.3, 3.5.

## IV. Discussion and Conclusion

### Case 15: After repeating a result, you must add meaning

Results:

> Pressure increased the rate by 23% under condition Y.

An effective Discussion restart:

> The present study showed that pressure increased the rate by 23% under condition Y. This finding indicates that pressure-sensitive control may be relevant to applications in which condition Y is unavoidable.

An ineffective repetition:

> In conclusion, pressure increased the rate by 23% under condition Y.

The Discussion may repeat key words, but after the repetition it must move on to meaning, literature comparison, limitation or application. Otherwise it merely reads the Results again.

### Case 16: Limiting a novelty claim

Safer:

> To our knowledge, this is the first study to compare X and Y under condition Z.

Higher risk:

> This is the first study ever to compare X and Y.

to our knowledge is not a weakening of the contribution, but a statement that the novelty judgement depends on the scope of the author's search. Absolute words such as first, largest and only should all be checked for support by a complete search or comparable data.

### Case 17: A recommendation vs a promise

A recommendation to the field:

> Future work should examine whether the same pattern occurs in larger populations.

A plan the author has already committed to:

> Future work will examine whether the same pattern occurs in larger populations.

should is a recommendation; will implies the author will do it personally. Using will without a clear plan adds an unnecessary commitment.

### Case 18: The evidence strength of may and must

Preserving an alternative explanation:

> The discrepancy may have been caused by differences in sample preparation.

Usable only after other explanations are ruled out:

> The discrepancy must have been caused by differences in sample preparation.

must is not a more forceful ordinary synonym, but a judgement that almost rules out alternative explanations. If there are still reasonable instrumental, sample or analytical explanations, must exceeds the evidence.

### Case 19: Making conclusion ownership explicit in the Conclusion

Potentially vague:

> Kinetic analyses suggest that the reaction proceeds through pathway X.

The authors' explicit synthetic judgement:

> We have synthesized the data and kinetic analyses to show that the reaction proceeds through pathway X.

Or:

> This study demonstrates that the reaction proceeds through pathway X.

The first sentence may make the "analytical tool" the subject of the conclusion, or may obscure the authors' judgement. The latter two make the contribution explicit, but demonstrates may only be used when the evidence is strong enough.

Source: Unit 4–5, 4.2.2, 4.5, 5.4.2.

## V. Abstract and Title

### Case 20: Result ownership in the Abstract

Unclear ownership:

> Analysis shows that the treatment changes the response.

Clearer:

> In this study, analysis showed that the treatment changed the response.

Or:

> These results show that the treatment changes the response.

Analysis shows may be read as a general law, as the output of a current analytical procedure, or as a result just obtained in this paper. Adding In this study, making the subject explicit, and distinguishing showed from show can reduce ambiguity.

### Case 21: The Abstract cannot be compressed into losing its logic

With a logic chain:

> Although X is important, Y remains unclear. We used method Z to examine Y. The results showed A, suggesting B.

A telegraphic counter-example:

> X is important. Y is unclear. Z was used. A was found. B is discussed.

Each sentence in the latter may be correct, yet it does not show the relation between problem, method, result and meaning. A short abstract still needs to preserve the minimal structure of "problem - action - finding - meaning".

### Case 22: A work title vs an output title

A work title:

> Mapping single-cell gene expression in the lung

A clear tool output:

> LungGENS: a web-based tool for mapping single-cell gene expression in the lung

The former states the research action and object; the latter tells the reader the output is a usable tool. The title should deliver the actual deliverable of the whole article, and must not use a tool-type title to package an ordinary analysis that does not provide a tool.

### Case 23: Scope of modification in the Title

Ambiguous:

> X-ray imaging was used to study Y for the first time.

for the first time may modify "using X-ray imaging", or may modify "studying Y".

Clearer:

> For the first time, X-ray imaging was used to study Y.

Or:

> X-ray imaging was used to study Y, which had not previously been examined with this method.

When checking a title, rewrite it as a full sentence and confirm the target of each preposition and modifier one by one.

Source: Unit 6–7, 6.1.1, 6.3, 7.4–7.6.

## VI. Sentence linking and language

### Case 24: The referential scope of this result

Potentially vague:

> The treatment reduced the response in sample A but increased it in sample B. This result was unexpected. This suggests that the treatment is context-dependent.

This result may refer to the overall result, the decrease in A, the increase in B, or even the judgement that it was "unexpected".

Explicit writing:

> The increase observed in sample B was unexpected. This sample-specific pattern suggests that the treatment is context-dependent.

A repeated noun is usually preferable to an ambiguous pronoun in a technical paper.

### Case 25: The relation of with

Potentially unclear:

> The samples were analysed with the new instrument.

Clearer:

> The samples were analysed using the new instrument.

with may indicate instrument, accompanying condition or material; using makes clear that the instrument is the analytical tool. A key method relation should not depend on the reader guessing.

### Case 26: evidence of vs evidence for

> evidence of contamination

Usually indicates evidence that contamination is present.

> evidence for the proposed mechanism

Usually indicates evidence that supports the proposition of a mechanism.

Swapping of and for may turn "a sign of the mechanism" into "evidence supporting the mechanism". A preposition check should look at the direction of the argument, not just whether the collocation is common.

### Case 27: The logical subject of an -ing form

Potentially vague:

> The ions are coordinated by the C2 domain and by the phospholipids forming a ternary complex.

forming may modify phospholipids, or may be misread by the reader as the action of the ions or of the whole preceding sentence.

Clearer:

> The ions are coordinated by the C2 domain and by the phospholipids; together, these components form a ternary complex.

If the agent of an -ing phrase is not the adjacent noun, split the sentence or supply the agent.

### Case 28: The position of only

> Only we analysed the models.

Only we did the analysis.

> We only analysed the models.

We only did the analysis, and did nothing else.

> We analysed only the models.

The object of our analysis was only the models.

only should be placed as close as possible to the element it limits. Placing it before the verb often gives the reader one interpretation first, to be corrected at the end of the sentence.

### Case 29: The paradox of must - certainty without evidence

> The discrepancy must have been caused by differences in sample preparation.

> The discrepancy was caused by differences in sample preparation.

> The discrepancy may have been caused by differences in sample preparation.

`must have been caused` means you are certain from reasoning, but cannot directly observe or provide empirical evidence. `was caused` states the fact directly and implies evidence. `may have been caused` preserves uncertainty. If you have evidence, stating it directly is stronger than using must; if you lack evidence but have logically ruled out other possibilities, must is appropriate but precisely signals the absence of evidence.

Check questions:
- Do I have direct evidence for this cause? If so, state it directly rather than using must.
- Am I using must because I have ruled out other explanations, or because I want the conclusion to seem stronger?
- Does must inadvertently expose my lack of empirical evidence?

### Case 30: can not vs cannot

> This system can not identify all variants.

> This system cannot identify all variants.

`cannot` means "impossible" (the system is not able to identify). `can not` (written separately) means "possibly not" (the system may not be able to identify, but it is uncertain). One space completely changes the meaning. In science writing you almost always mean "impossible", so write `cannot`.

### Case 31: The dual meaning of should

> The ratio should remain constant.

> The tubes should be centrifuged at 3000 rpm.

The should in the first sentence means "expected/likely" (is expected to). The should in the second sentence means "advisable/recommended" (it is advisable to). The same word, two completely different meanings. The reader must judge which one from context. If the context cannot distinguish them, replace with `is expected to` or `it is advisable to`.

### Case 32: A hyphen changes the scope of modification

> five centimetre-wide layers

> five-centimetre wide layers

The first = five layers, each one centimetre wide. The second = each layer five centimetres wide. The position of the hyphen determines whether "five" modifies "layers" or "centimetres". When checking a hyphen, rewrite the phrase as a full sentence and confirm the modification.

### Case 33: The referential scope of we

> We can now distinguish between these two mechanisms.

> It is now possible to distinguish between these two mechanisms.

The `we` in the first sentence may refer to the authors of this paper, or to researchers in the whole field. If the meaning is "everyone in the field can now distinguish", the second sentence is more accurate. If the meaning is "the authors of this paper developed a method that now makes the distinction possible", lock down `we` in the context (e.g. `In this study, we developed a method that can now distinguish...`).

Source: Unit 4 §4.5 (modal verbs); Unit 8 §8.3 (hyphens, we reference).

## VII. Turning cases into check records

| Item | What to record |
|---|---|
| Original sentence | Keep the author's original intent; do not rewrite first |
| Contrast sentence | Write the minimal variant that gives another possible meaning |
| Minimal difference | Tense, modal, preposition, only, evaluative word or referential word |
| Reader risk | Possible misreading of conclusion ownership, causal relation, numerical meaning or scope of modification |
| Scientific intent | The fact, scope and evidence strength the author actually wants to express |
| Fix action | Change a word, supply an agent, split a sentence or reorder a paragraph |
| Recheck question | Turn this finding into a repeatable check item |

Prioritise checking these locations:

- The gap, this paper's aim and novelty claims in the Introduction;
- Step conditions, method modifications and experimental problems in Methods;
- Numerical evaluation, figure interpretation and causal wording in Results;
- Mechanism explanation, limitations and future work in Discussion/Conclusion;
- Ownership, scope and logical compression in the Abstract and Title.

A paragraph that is grammatically flawless but lacks a research map cannot be polished into an effective argument; a sentence whose science is correct but whose tense, reference or modal is vague may also cause unnecessary misreading in review.
