# 07 Abstract: The independent, clear and discoverable high-stakes text

## 1. Section mission

The Abstract is read, scanned or forwarded far more often than the full text. It must function as a standalone text that lets the reader quickly judge what the research did, what it found and what its value is. The word limit is a boundary, not a target to be filled; overloading makes the central contribution disappear.

## 2. Types

- Simple/Standard Abstract;
- Structured Abstract: with headings such as Background/Objective/Methods/Results/Conclusions;
- Abstract + Significance Statement/Highlights;
- Graphical Abstract.

The type may vary, but the function chain does not.

## 3. The three-band model

```text
A1 Significance of the topic/essential factual background + challenge/problem + what the paper/study does
A2 Method/materials + results/comparisons + necessary implications
A3 Mapping to existing knowledge + achievement/value/contribution + applications
```

Note the potential locations for "happy words" that communicate the value of the study quickly and unmistakably.

## 3.1 Systems-conference variant (governs this project; see guideline 14, SYS-11)

For a systems paper the three-band model specializes to a fixed function order in one paragraph, typically 150–250 words within the venue limit:

```text
problem/pressure (with a number if possible)
-> why existing systems fail at it
-> the key insight of this paper
-> the system by name and what it does (its 2-3 core mechanisms)
-> 2-4 concrete result numbers, each with evaluative direction
-> scope / availability of the artifact
```

A systems abstract without concrete numbers is a red flag to reviewers; every number keeps the evaluative-direction rule (ABST-08 / RES-04). Structured abstracts and graphical abstracts are journal devices and are not used.

## 4. Rules

### ABST-01 Write the Abstract last, then refocus for the target journal

- Rule strength: NORMALLY.
- Writing action: After the body of the paper is stable, select the central achievement, key method, key results and significance from the actual content; before submission, review and if necessary refocus according to the target journal.
- Why: Writing too early risks inconsistency with the final paper; different journals have different structures, word limits, and requirements for Significance and Highlights. The Abstract derives from the paper, not the other way around.
- Completion criterion: Every fact in the Abstract has a counterpart in the body; the central contribution of the body is prioritised in the Abstract.
- Source: Unit 6 §6.1.1.

### ABST-02 Use the minimum background needed to establish the reader's starting point

- Rule strength: CONDITIONAL.
- Writing action: For a broad or interdisciplinary readership, add one or two sentences of essential background; for a specialist microcommunity, compress the background; do not move the Introduction's entire literature review into the Abstract.
- Citations: Abstracts normally contain no citations; consider one only if the article follows directly from, is a major advance on, or directly contradicts a specific work.
- Completion criterion: The reader can understand the problem without consulting external literature, while the central contribution is not squeezed out by background.
- Source: Unit 6 §6.1.1, §6.3.1.

### ABST-03 Explicitly mark the authors' contribution to eliminate ownership ambiguity

- Rule strength: MUST.
- Writing action: Use Past Simple/Present Perfect, the active `we`, `In this study`, `Here`, or an explicit study subject; do not let a non-human (agentless passive) subject in the Present Simple look like an established field fact.
- Contrast: `Analysis shows...` may be unclear who carried out the analysis; `Analysis showed...` more clearly points to work done in this study.
- Why: When the verb is in the Present Simple, the information may seem to be well known, whereas it may in fact be a description of your results. Your aim is not to make it possible for the reader to understand the Abstract; it is to make it impossible for the reader not to understand it AND identify your contribution.
- Completion criterion: The reader can distinguish background, the paper's method, the paper's results and the paper's interpretation.
- Source: Unit 6 §6.1.1, Unit 5 §5.4.2.

### ABST-04 Keep terminology consistent and define abbreviations on first use

- Rule strength: MUST.
- Writing action: Always call the same object by the same word; if an acronym is necessary, write the full form once on first use; use few acronyms that are transparent only to a microcommunity.
- Why: The Abstract is read independently; terminology drift makes the reader think there are multiple objects, and acronyms directly block search and reading.
- Completion criterion: No scheme/approach/framework/method swapped at will, and no undefined key abbreviations.
- Source: Unit 6 §6.1.1.

### ABST-05 Increase information density by combining sentence functions without breaking the narrative

- Rule strength: NORMALLY.
- Combinable: what the paper does + achievement; method + result; result + implication; achievement + gap.
- Sentence/paragraph skeleton: `We present [novel approach] that [achievement].` / `[Result] ..., suggesting that [implication].`
- Common errors: Deleting all inter-sentence links to cut word count, producing a data list.
- Completion criterion: Each sentence carries enough information and allows the reader to predict the next sentence without supplying the logic themselves.
- Source: Unit 6 §6.3.1.

### ABST-06 Keep results, implications and strength consistent with the body

- Rule strength: MUST.
- Writing action: If the work is early-stage evidence or a possible application, use may/might/could, possible/potential; if the data are sufficient, you may use stronger but verifiable words; never exceed what the body supports in order to attract the reader.
- Completion criterion: The reviewer can find the evidence and hedging for every strong claim of the Abstract in the body.
- Source: Unit 6 §6.3.1.

### ABST-07 Choose tense by component

- Rule strength: NORMALLY.
- What the paper does/paper structure: Present Simple (`This paper presents...`).
- Method (what was done/used): Past Simple (`We used...` / `X was measured...`).
- Results/implications: Past Simple or Present Simple; the Present Simple may be used in the Abstract even if the body uses the Past Simple, to engage attention and communicate current relevance.
- Achievement/value/contribution: Present Perfect (`We have developed...`) or Present Simple (`This study demonstrates...`).
- Completion criterion: Each tense choice can be explained as either "what the research did in the past" or "what it is now taken to mean."
- Source: Unit 6 §6.3.1.

### ABST-08 Control sentence length and word count

- Rule strength: NORMALLY.
- Typical Abstract length: 80–250 words, a single paragraph. The word limit is not a target - do not pad with irrelevant content to fill it.
- Sentence length and comprehension: Sentences with fewer than 20 words are understood by 90% of readers at first reading; those with more than 40 words are understood by 10% of readers at first reading. The average sentence length in the sample Abstracts is about 22 words.
- A run of long sentences "flattens" the important information, with the main clause buried in nouns and prepositions.
- Completion criterion: The average sentence length is consistent with the target journal's Abstracts; there are no sentences over 40 words (unless there is a good reason).
- Source: Unit 6 §6.1.1.

### ABST-09 Numbers in the Abstract must also have an evaluative direction

- Rule strength: NORMALLY.
- Writing action: When reporting key numbers in the Abstract, add evaluative language (e.g. `only 38%`, `as high as 38%`, `a substantial reduction`) so the reader knows whether the number is high or low.
- Why: The Abstract is read independently; the reader has no body context to judge the relative significance of a number, and a naked number will be read as high by some readers and low by others.
- Completion criterion: Every key number in the Abstract has an evaluative direction tied to the research question.
- Source: Unit 6 §6.3.1.

## 5. Additional text by type

- **Significance Statement**: Explains why the research matters to the field or the wider world, highlighting the perspective, the impact and the message the reader can take away.
- **Highlights**: Short result/contribution sentences listing the main findings; they should not be mere keywords, nor should they introduce conclusions not in the Abstract.
- **Graphical Abstract**: A single-panel, low-clutter, visually prioritised take-home message; colour and elements serve immediate understanding and retention.

## 6. Abstract completion check

- Can it be independently understood for the research problem, method, results and value?
- Is one central achievement prioritised, rather than listing all the outputs?
- Is the authors' contribution explicitly owned?
- Is the key method sufficient for the reader to judge the research type and credibility?
- Do the key results carry the necessary quantity and evaluation?
- Are the implications consistent with the evidence strength in the body?
- Are there overlapping sentences, pronouns or this/it reference ambiguity?
- Do the terminology, abbreviations, tenses and average sentence length match the target journal?


## Worked examples from the book: compression must not sacrifice ownership

### Counter-example: unclear result subject

> Analysis shows that the treatment changes the response.

### Good example: explicit study ownership and time

> In this study, analysis showed that the treatment changed the response.

or:

> These results show that the treatment changes the response.

`Analysis shows` may refer to general analytical capability, the output of a current procedure, or this study's results. Adding `In this study`, using `showed`, or specifying `These results` lets the reader know who obtained what.

### Good example: preserving the minimal logic chain

> Although X is important, Y remains unclear. We used method Z to examine Y. The results showed A, suggesting B.

### Counter-example: telegraphic information stacking

> X is important. Y is unclear. Z was used. A was found. B is discussed.

The counter-example fails not because the sentences are short, but because it deletes the relationships between problem, method, result and meaning. The goal of Abstract compression is to reduce repetition, not to delete logical connections.

### Terminology and reference check

If the earlier text says treatment, the later text should not switch to intervention, procedure or approach merely to avoid repetition, unless the scope genuinely changes. If `this result` in a sentence could refer to two results, repeat the key noun directly. The Abstract is short; the reader does not have enough context to repair ambiguity for the author.

### Check questions

- Does the background serve only the research problem?
- Does the method state how it answers the question?
- Do the results give a specific finding, rather than only "analysis was performed"?
- Does the conclusion state the significance with restraint?
- Is the referent of each this, it, analysis, result unambiguous?

Source: Unit 6, 6.1.1 and 6.3.
