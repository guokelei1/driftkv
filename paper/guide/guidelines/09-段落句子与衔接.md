# 09 Paragraphs, Sentences and Linking

> Genre note: the rules in this document are genre-independent and apply unchanged to systems-conference papers. Where "target articles" is mentioned, calibrate against recent papers from the target venue.

## 1. The paragraph as a narrative unit

### PARA-01 A paragraph normally has a single central function

- Rule strength: MUST.
- Possible functions: provide background, explain the advantage of a method, compare studies, interpret results, summarise implications, point out a limitation, or introduce the next step.
- Every sentence and every clause should advance that function; a "loose sentence" that does not serve the function should be deleted, moved, or wrapped in a narrative that respects the function.
- Average paragraph length can be calibrated against target articles; the book offers the observation that in most research articles paragraphs are about 150–170 words, but this is not a hard rule.
- Error: consecutive single-sentence paragraphs break up the reading; an over-long paragraph starts on one topic and ends on another.
- Source: Unit 1 §1.5.4; Unit 8 §8.1.

### PARA-02 Use an entry sentence to give the reader a small map

- Rule strength: NORMALLY.
- Writing action: state the topic, action, purpose or relation to the previous paragraph at the head of the paragraph; then develop, define, exemplify, evidence or explain it.
- Slots: `To address this question, we...`, `These data suggest that...`, `An alternative approach is...`, `We now consider...`
- Completion criterion: reading only the first sentence of each paragraph reveals the order of the paragraphs and the direction of the article.
- Source: Unit 1 §1.5.4; Unit 8 §8.1.

### PARA-03 Order information within the paragraph by the reader's processing order

1. The starting point the reader already knows or must know;
2. The new claim or action of this paragraph;
3. Evidence, examples, explanation or conditions;
4. A wrap of the paragraph's function and an interface to the next paragraph.

Do not place a conclusion early that will only make sense once the whole paragraph has been read; if giving the conclusion first creates a dangling thread, reverse the order.

## 2. Four ways to link sentences

### LINK-01 Overlap (repeat)

Repeat an important word from the previous sentence at the start of the next one:

```text
...the transition into unconsciousness. Unconsciousness can occur...
...the defined substrata. Such defined substrata have been developed...
```

Repetition is not poverty of language; it builds a conceptual echo for the reader, which is especially helpful for global readers and technical terms.

### LINK-02 Pro-form + explicit noun

`This method`, `These results`, `This shortcoming` etc. carry over from the previous sentence, but do not write only `This`, `It`, `They` and force the reader to guess the referent. If the term is device, keep using `this device`; do not arbitrarily turn it into technique, method or approach.

### LINK-03 Semicolon

When two sentences are very close and the shorter one explains the fact of the previous one, a semicolon may be used; check the overall sentence length, and avoid using a semicolon to mask two separate paragraph functions.

### LINK-04 Signalling connectors

| Relation | Common signals |
|---|---|
| Cause | due to, because, since |
| Result | therefore, consequently, hence, as a result |
| Contrast/difference | however, whereas, by contrast |
| Unexpected | nevertheless, nonetheless, yet |
| Addition | moreover, furthermore, in addition |
| Transition | regarding, with respect to, turning now to |

Signals are not decorative glue. `therefore` requires a clear and direct cause, `for example` must really exemplify, and `moreover` must carry the same function as the previous sentence.

## 3. Known information to new information

Sentences normally place known information first as a platform and new information later. The next sentence then turns that new information into known information. If new information is always buried in the middle or at the end of the sentence while the next sentence suddenly changes topic, the reader loses the path.

### PARA-04 Sentence start-up frames the reader's processing

- Rule strength: MUST.
- Writing action: the start of a sentence is the frame by which the reader processes the whole sentence; make sure the start can (a) carry over known information from the previous sentence, (b) signal the function of this sentence, and (c) not create ambiguity.
- **Do not begin sentences with `-ing` forms**: an `-ing` form shows no tense and no subject, so the reader cannot tell who is performing the action or how it relates to the previous sentence. If you must use one, convert it to an explicit clause (`When monitoring the temperature, we...` rather than `Monitoring the temperature, ...`).
- **Do not begin sentences with prepositions (especially `for` and `with`)**: `From this estimation, we ranked...` is less clear than `Using this estimation, we ranked...`. Sentences that start with a preposition often lose the reader before the subject is reached.
- Use sentence openers such as `Interestingly,` / `Remarkably,` / `It is noteworthy that` / `Importantly,` to signal the importance of the sentence.
- Completion criterion: every sentence start lets the reader immediately know the relation of this sentence to the previous one and the function of this sentence.
- Source: Unit 8 §8.2.

## 4. Sentence length, density and splitting

- Target articles are usually around 20–26 words; the book observes that sentences with fewer than 20 words are easier to process at one reading, while sentences with more than 40 words are more likely to require re-reading.
- Consecutive long sentences make the important information "flat"; the main clause becomes submerged in nouns and prepositions.
- High-risk structures: multiple `and`, multiple `which`, strings of prepositions, too much nominalisation, passive verbs dragged to the end of the sentence.
- `and/or` may drag in things you do not want, or fail to drag in things you do want; if the scope matters, list the options explicitly.
- Revision action: first locate the main verb; split off time, purpose and condition; turn long noun strings into verbs; use two shorter sentences with an explicit link.

## 5. Pronoun, reference and implicit-linkage checks

- Add a noun after `this/these/it/which` when necessary: `This result`, `This limitation`, `This method`.
- Check whether `which` modifies the preceding word, the whole fact, or the result of the whole sentence.
- When a sentence contains two possible referents, repeating the noun is clearer than striving for brevity.
- Can the reader tell whether `we` means the authors of this paper, researchers in the whole field, or people in general? If not, rewrite.

## 6. Sentence role table

| Role | Main action | Common location |
|---|---|---|
| Background sentence | Gives a shared fact or definition | Start of Introduction/Methods/Discussion |
| Scope sentence | Limits object, time, reader or condition | Before or after a claim |
| Claim sentence | States what the author wants the reader to believe | Paragraph start or before evidence |
| Evidence sentence | Gives data, literature, method or figure | Immediately after the claim |
| Evaluation sentence | Marks size, direction, importance or reliability | Results/Discussion |
| Explanation sentence | Says why or how it arose | Results/Discussion |
| Comparison sentence | Places the result in literature, model or control | Introduction/Results/Discussion |
| Limitation sentence | States unmeasured factors, anomalies or boundaries | Methods/Results/Discussion |
| Transition sentence | Hands the current function over to the next | End of paragraph or section |

## 7. Paragraph/sentence completion check

- Can each paragraph be named with a single verb phrase?
- Does the paragraph start give direction and does the end hand over to the next paragraph?
- If each sentence is removed, does some specific logical function disappear?
- Is there evidence, reason or a strength qualifier next to every claim?
- Does the sentence start carry over from the previous sentence, or does it change topic abruptly in the gap of the author's thinking?
- Are there loose sentences retained only because they came from lab notes or a colleague's suggestion?
