# 10 Tense, Voice, Mood and Research Language

> Genre note: the rules in this document are genre-independent and apply unchanged to systems-conference papers. Two calibrations for systems writing: (a) the active voice with `we` is the default and dominant style at systems venues — use the passive only where the agent is genuinely irrelevant; (b) system behavior that holds whenever the system runs ("the compiler folds the residual into the projection") takes the Present Simple, while what was measured in the experiments takes the Past Simple; do not let the Present Simple of system description leak into unmeasured performance claims.

## 1. Tense is a choice of meaning, not just of grammar

### LANG-01 Past Simple and Present Simple

- Past Simple: binds the finding to a completed study or a particular observation: `We found that X was...`.
- Present Simple: presents the finding as a current, reliable, stable or general fact: `We found that X is...`; it has more power, but also higher risk.
- Background facts commonly take the Present Simple; research activity and specific methods commonly take the Past Simple; but each field should be calibrated against recent target articles.
- Completion criterion: every tense change can answer "what do I want the reader to take this sentence as".

### LANG-02 Present Perfect signals current relevance

- `has been studied` means the research has developed up to the present and still matters; `was studied` only places the action in the past.
- In the Introduction, moving from the Past Simple of an earlier result to the Present Perfect of a current gap tells the reader that the gap still exists and will be addressed by this article.
- Do not switch tense randomly; random switching changes the time and status of the fact.

### LANG-03 Do not use tense to mask insufficient evidence

The Present Simple can strengthen a conclusion, but it cannot disguise a single result as a universal law. When evidence is insufficient, use an appropriate qualifier such as `appears to`, `may`, `in some cases`, or keep the Past Simple.

## 2. Active, passive and ownership

### LANG-04 Choose voice by style and ownership

- Active `we investigated`: the authors' action is clear, suitable for marking this paper's contribution;
- Passive `samples were collected`: focuses on the material or process, suitable for the conventional Methods style of the target journal;
- Impersonal subject `This study demonstrates...`: avoids repeating `we`, but may still need to make this paper's ownership explicit.

Do not choose mechanically on the grounds that "science writing must be passive". The target journal and the risk of ambiguity take priority.

### LANG-05 Resolve the three kinds of ambiguity of the agentless passive

Sentence by sentence, determine whether it is: your own work, the work of others, a standard procedure, your own content in a figure, or your own work where the journal prefers the Present Simple. Correspondingly use `in this study`, `in their work`, `using standard procedures`, `the setup used here`, or move to the active.

## 3. Modal verbs and the certainty continuum

| Meaning | Common structures | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Able | can, be able to, could | `could` can also mean possible; use be able to when necessary |
| Possible | may, might, could, it is possible that | might is usually weaker than may; cannot treat could not as possibly not |
| Expected/likely | should, be expected to, likely to | should can also mean advisable |
| Obvious/impossible | must, cannot, must have, cannot have | must means certainty inferred from reasoning, not equal to directly observed |
| Advisable/recommended | should, ought to | mainly for method recommendations and future work |
| Necessary/essential | must, need to, have to | note that the negative forms differ in meaning |

Before choosing a word, fix your position on the certainty continuum: proof, strong evidence, a reasonable explanation, a possible explanation, or pure speculation. Do not use may everywhere just to "stay safe"; over-weakening also harms the paper.

### LANG-06 High-risk traps of modal verbs

The following traps change sentence meaning and must be checked individually in a sentence-by-sentence review:

- **`must` = certainty without direct evidence**: `The drop in pressure must have been caused by a crack` means you are certain from reasoning, but cannot directly observe or provide empirical evidence. Being certain that something is true and knowing that it is true are not the same; `must` precisely implies an absence of empirical evidence. If the evidence is sufficient, state it directly rather than using must.
- **`can` has three meanings**: `Particle formation can occur in the boundary layer` may mean (a) it may occur (may), (b) it is able to occur (be able to), (c) it sometimes occurs (sometimes). When ambiguous, replace with `it is possible that`, `is able to` or `sometimes`.
- **`can not` ≠ `cannot`**: `cannot` means "impossible"; `can not` (written separately) means "possibly not" (equivalent to may not). The space completely changes the meaning.
- **`must not` ≠ "not necessary"**: `must not` means "not allowed" (prohibited), not "not necessary". "Not necessary" should be written `do not need to` / `need not` / `do not have to`.
- **`should` has two meanings**: (a) expected/likely (`The ratio should remain constant` = is expected to); (b) advisable/recommended (`The tubes should be centrifuged` = it is advisable to). The context must be able to distinguish the two.
- **`could not` / `cannot` / `cannot have` / `could not have` all mean "impossible"**, and cannot be used to mean "possibly not". For "possibly not" use only `may not` / `might not`.
- **`should have` usually refers to something that did not happen**; `should not have` usually refers to something that did happen.
- **Trend**: an increasing number of papers use alternative structures (`it is possible that`, `it is likely that`) instead of modal verbs, possibly because modal meanings are complex and easily misused.

### LANG-07 Do not over-weaken the paper out of excessive caution

Implication statements in the Discussion should not over-use may/might/could to "stay safe". If the results and existing knowledge support a stronger interpretation, over-weakening undervalues the research and may also be questioned in review. Language strength must match the strength of the results: use stronger expressions when the evidence is strong, and use modal verbs only when the evidence is weak.

Source: Unit 3 §3.5; Unit 4 §4.5; Unit 8 §8.3–8.4.

## 4. Causal verbs and direction

- Strong cause: cause, produce, result in;
- Partial cause: contribute to, be a factor in;
- Process relation: lead to, give rise to;
- Non-causal link: be related to, linked to, associated with;
- Direction: `X results from Y` means X is the result of Y; `X results in Y` means Y is the result of X.

Articles also change strength: `a cause/result` allows other factors to exist; `the cause/result` implies a unique factor.

## 5. Precise use of linking words

Write the relation first, then choose the connector. Check in particular:

- `however` marks a difference or something unexpected, not a decoration for every paragraph;
- `therefore` needs a cause visible on the page;
- `moreover` must add information of the same function;
- `for example` must be a genuine example of the previous sentence;
- `as` may mean because or when; if ambiguous, change to because/when;
- `since` may mean cause or a starting time.

## 6. Prepositions, articles, -ing and vocabulary

- Prepositions carry content meaning; they are not glue for sticking nouns together. Prefer stable verb + preposition clusters from target articles such as `using`, `by + -ing`, `for`, `at`, `on`, `under`.
- `with` may mean using, having, at the same time as, as a result of, or intention; replace it with a clearer word when possible.
- Avoid strings of prepositional phrases; split the sentence when necessary. Strings of prepositional phrases "infect" each other and create muddled modification (e.g. `The tray with the samples was placed in the oven at 250°C with protective gloves to avoid injury for one hour`).
- `a/an`, zero article and `the` reflect first introduction, indefiniteness, general fact and shared knowledge; do not decide mechanically by "general vs specific".
- Avoid overly broad verbs such as have, get, bring, keep, spread; use specific research verbs at critical steps.
- Do not force-replace terms with a thesaurus. Repeating the same word can help the reader track a concept and keep the concept stable.

### LANG-08 -ing forms are a high-risk area for ambiguity

An `-ing` form shows no tense and no subject, and may carry more than ten logical relations. Common interpretations include:

| Possible meaning | Example |
|---|---|
| by (through) | `using the new instrument` |
| as a result of | `resulting in a higher yield` |
| on the basis of | `based on these findings` |
| when/while | `monitoring the temperature` |
| thereby | `producing a stable compound` |
| therefore | - |
| which is capable of | `forming a ternary complex` |
| which (relative pronoun) | - |
| if | - |
| because | - |

Checking action:
- If the logical subject of the `-ing` phrase is not the adjacent noun, split the sentence or supply the subject.
- **Do not begin sentences with `-ing` forms** — sentence start-up frames the reader's processing, and an `-ing` opener leaves the reader unable to judge the subject and the function.
- Counter-example: `The ions are coordinated by the C2 domain and by the phospholipids forming a ternary complex.` (what does forming modify?)
- Correct example: `The ions are coordinated by the C2 domain and by the phospholipids; together, these components form a ternary complex.`

Source: Unit 8 §8.2–8.3.

### LANG-09 The referential ambiguity of `we`

`we/us/our` may refer to the authors of this paper, or to researchers in the whole field. The reader cannot always tell from context. If the meaning is "everyone in the field", use `It is now possible to...` or `Researchers in this field...`; if it refers to the authors of this paper, make sure the context (e.g. `In this study, we...`) locks it down.

### LANG-10 Subject-verb agreement and logical subject

- When multiple nouns intervene between the subject and the verb, check singular/plural agreement.
- Check whether the subject can logically perform the action of the verb: `The nature of the problem decided how to resolve it` — a "nature" cannot "decide".
- Do not place a passive verb at the end of a long sentence — the reader has to wait a long time to find out what happened.

### LANG-11 The ambiguity of and/or

`and` may drag in things you do not want, and `or` may leave out things you do want. `A and/or B` is particularly dangerous in a technical context. If the scope matters, list all options explicitly or use `either A or B` / `both A and B`.

### LANG-12 Easily confused word pairs

| Pair | Distinction |
|---|---|
| alternately / alternatively | alternately = one after the other, in sequence; alternatively = on the other hand, instead |
| beside / besides | beside = next/close to; besides = apart from, in addition to |
| criterion / criteria | criterion is singular; criteria is plural |
| different / various | different = not the same; various = a range of |
| e.g. / i.e. | e.g. = for example; i.e. = in other words |
| effective / efficient | effective = it works; efficient = it works well |
| phenomenon / phenomena | phenomenon is singular; phenomena is plural |
| adapt / adopt | adapt = to modify/adjust; adopt = to choose to use/follow |
| affect / effect | affect (verb) = to influence; effect (noun) = result |
| imply / infer | imply = to suggest/indicate (speaker sends); infer = to conclude/deduce (listener/reader draws) |
| comprise / include | comprise / consist of = all components; include = a selection of the components |
| respectively | = in the same order as just mentioned |

### LANG-13 Hyphens change the scope of modification

A hyphen joins two or more words into a single unit that describes the following noun. Different hyphen placement gives a completely different meaning:

- `five centimetre-wide layers` = five layers, each one centimetre wide
- `five-centimetre wide layers` = each layer five centimetres wide

When checking, rewrite the hyphenated phrase as a full sentence to confirm the modification is correct.

### LANG-14 Self-edit: clear out unconscious habits

- **Lexical tics**: check whether `indeed`, `in fact`, `basically`, `clearly` actually add information, or are just verbal habits.
- **Pairs of dashes**: the meaning is not obvious, especially to international readers; translate the dash content into explicit language.
- **Parentheses**: if the content is important enough to include, perhaps it should not be in parentheses; if it is not important enough, perhaps it should not be there at all.
- **Comma stacking**: using commas to keep adding information creates a rambling, over-long sentence; break it into shorter sentences with explicit links.

## 7. Language quality check

- Is it accurate, concise, specific and unambiguous?
- Are technical terms consistent with current usage in the field?
- Is the same object always referred to by the same name?
- Do evaluative words have evidence, rather than being based on feeling?
- Have nominalisations, long prepositional strings and passive structures been broken apart?
- Has unnecessary language burden been reduced for global readers and possible interdisciplinary readers?
- Have modal verbs avoided the high-risk traps (must = certainty without evidence, the three meanings of can, can not ≠ cannot, must not ≠ not necessary, the dual meaning of should)?
- Is the logical subject of `-ing` forms clear? Does any sentence begin with -ing?
- Does `we` refer to the authors of this paper rather than the whole field?
- Does hyphen placement change the scope of modification?
- Are there unconscious lexical tics (indeed/in fact/basically/clearly)?
- Are easily confused word pairs used correctly?
