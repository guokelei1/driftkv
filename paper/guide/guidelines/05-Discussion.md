# 05 Discussion: Interpreting, Positioning and Delivering the Research Contribution

## 1. Section mission

The Discussion puts the Results back into the map of existing knowledge and research, answering "what do these results mean, what has this paper advanced, where are the boundaries, and what could happen next." It must not simply restate the results, nor should it present unsupported speculation as conclusions.

**Systems-paper mapping (governs this project).** Systems-conference papers usually have **no standalone Discussion section**. The Discussion functions distribute as follows (guideline 14 SYS-01):

- interpretation and comparison with prior systems → inside each Evaluation RQ subsection, next to the evidence;
- limitations, scope statements and threats to validity → a final "Discussion and limitations" subsection of the Evaluation (or a short section before Related Work);
- positioning on the research map → the Related Work section (SYS-09);
- achievement/contribution delivery → the Introduction's contribution bullets and the Conclusion.

The rules below (destination-first, move-forward, echo language, not over-weakening, achievement vs contribution) apply unchanged to wherever this content lives.

## 2. The Introduction mirror model

```text
Introduction: the field -> existing research -> gap -> your paper
Discussion:   your paper -> your response to the gap -> research map -> contribution/impact
```

The Introduction starts out fairly general and gradually narrows towards the Method/Results, whereas the Discussion moves away from the Method/Results towards a wider focus. The two sections mirror each other.

## 3. Rules

### DISC-01 First decide on the destination of the Discussion

- Rule strength: NORMALLY.
- Writing action: Before planning the Discussion, step back from the study and decide which category the main value or contribution belongs to: a better method, more accurate results, a change of research direction, an extended application, or an additional layer of knowledge.
- Why: Researchers typically spend less than 30 minutes reading a research article; without an early destination, the Discussion will be carried away by irrelevant detail. Setting a clear destination ensures the take-home message will not get lost.
- Completion criterion: The opening and the close of the Discussion can both be held together by the same central contribution.
- Source: Unit 4 §4.1.1.

### DISC-02 Open by delivering the achievement/contribution, or reboot the reader

- Rule strength: NORMALLY.
- Two entry points:
  - State the achievement, contribution or potential applications directly;
  - Revisit relevant background factual information, the gap, the aim, or key features of the method or results.
- **Choose what to revisit according to the type of main contribution**:
  - Main contribution is an improvement/new method -> revisit the difficulties associated with the existing method first;
  - Main contribution is more accurate/new results -> revisit your results first;
  - Main contribution is a strong response to the gap -> revisit that gap first.
- Sentence/paragraph skeleton: `To our knowledge, this is the first...` (keeps the knowledge scope on absolute claims) / `Our study provides evidence that...` / `Here, we show...`
- `to our knowledge` is not a sign of weakness; it acknowledges that research develops and is published at a very fast rate, and avoids an unnecessary absolute priority claim. Immediately before submission you should check current research as thoroughly as possible, using a wide range of keywords, so that comparable work is not overlooked.
- Why: Beginning the Discussion by stating the achievement, contribution or potential applications is a strong response to the type of information-surfing in which the reader has a simple question governing a fast and highly selective reading (e.g. "What exactly did this study manage to achieve?" or "What are the potential applications of these findings?"). Rebooting the reader also supports those who reached the Discussion directly from the Title/Abstract.
- Completion criterion: Within the first few sentences, the reader can answer "what exactly has this study achieved, and why is it valuable."
- Source: Unit 4 §4.2.2–4.2.3.

### DISC-03 Revisited results must "move forward"

- Rule strength: MUST.
- Writing action: You may repeat key results as an evidence anchor, but immediately add interpretation, comparison, significance, limitation or application; do not merely rewrite the Results.
- Sentence/paragraph skeleton: `Key result -> interpretation -> relation to prior knowledge -> implication.`
- Why: Without the key result, the Discussion's interpretation has no basis; but without "moving forward," the Discussion has no reason to exist. Saying what your results are is the central function of the Results section; going on to explore what they mean is the central function of the Discussion.
- Completion criterion: If you delete a sentence, ask what explicit connection between result and meaning would be lost; if nothing would be lost, the sentence is probably mere repetition.
- Source: Unit 4 §4.2.2.

### DISC-04 Map your study onto the research map

- Rule strength: MUST.
- Writing action: State where your study is consistent with, similar to, complementary to, extends, improves on, conflicts with or contradicts existing research; for every citation, make its function in the argument explicit to the reader via the narrative.
- Sentence/paragraph skeleton: `These results are consistent with...` / `This study extends previous work by...` / `Unlike existing approaches, ...` / `Our data provide support for...`
- Conditions and variants: You may cite a study in the Discussion for the first time if it mentions the interpretations of data or applications discussed in that study; the citation must be relevant and connected to the narrative.
- Completion criterion: The reader can say which step forward your study has taken within existing knowledge.
- Source: Unit 4 §4.2.2, §4.4.2.

### DISC-05 Distinguish achievement, contribution and impact

- achievement: what the study itself has successfully accomplished on its own research question; try to reuse the verb from the Introduction aim.
- contribution: what the study adds to knowledge, method or a research line.
- impact/application: how the real world, industry, the clinic, policy or subsequent research might use it.
- Rule strength: MUST distinguish the concepts conceptually, although the expression may overlap.
- Completion criterion: One sentence can answer "what this paper did," and another can answer "what capability or knowledge others thereby gained."
- Source: Unit 4 §4.2.3, §4.4.2.

### DISC-05b Use "echo" language when revisiting earlier sections; do not switch words

- Rule strength: MUST.
- Writing action: When revisiting the Introduction's gap or aim, or the Results' key findings in the Discussion, **deliberately repeat** the same vocabulary and sentence patterns instead of swapping in synonyms for the sake of style.
- Why: Repeating the same words and phrases creates an echo that helps the reader recall or find the earlier section. Changing the language places a burden on the reader to work out whether the new word refers to the same thing. If the Introduction aim used `identify`, the Discussion achievement should also use `identify`, not `find` or `detect`.
- Common errors: Changing framework to methodology, approach, or model "to avoid repetition" makes the reader think there are several different objects.
- Completion criterion: The key concept words in the Discussion are consistent with those in the Introduction/Results.
- Source: Unit 4 §4.4.2.

### DISC-05c Do not over-weaken implication statements

- Rule strength: MUST.
- Writing action: If the results and existing knowledge support a stronger interpretation, use language of matching strength; do not indiscriminately use may/might/could just to be "on the safe side."
- Why: Over-weakening undermines and diminishes the value of your work, and may be a point of criticism at peer review, since reviewers may think the author lacks confidence in the results. Equally, do not overstate; the language strength must match the power of the results and the scope supported by the design (see Section 3.5: The Certainty Continuum).
- Completion criterion: The strength of every implication statement can be supported by the study design and evidence; it neither exceeds nor falls below what the evidence allows.
- Source: Unit 4 §4.2.2, §4.5.

### DISC-06 Link limitations to specific directions for future research

- Rule strength: NORMALLY.
- Writing action: Point out the unaddressed questions, samples, methods or evidence boundaries, then give an actionable, reasonable future direction that arises from the problem of this paper.
- `future work should...` is a suggestion to the research community; `future work will...` indicates the author's own plan. The two must not be mixed.
- **"In-progress" phrases are not an invitation to others**: `further work is planned`, `work is currently underway`, `work is in progress` indicate that the writer is currently working on this; they are NOT suggestions for research by others. Do not mix them with `should`.
- Why: A specific direction is more likely to be carried out than "more research is needed," and encourages a line of direct continuity from your research.
- Conditions and variants: Limitations are normally mentioned for the first time in the relevant Methods or Results subsection where the problem first arises. Exceptions: anecdotal evidence, or a limitation linked to the need for further work that makes it difficult to commit to conclusions, may be mentioned in the Discussion and connected to a future direction.
- Completion criterion: The future research has an object, an action and a reason, and is not a new topic introduced from nowhere.
- Source: Unit 4 §4.2.2, §4.4.2.

### DISC-07 Applications may appear at the start or the end, but must be constrained by evidence

- Rule strength: CONDITIONAL.
- Writing action: If the application is itself the main contribution, state it clearly at the start; if the application is only an extension, place it towards the end; where there is no obvious application, the study may still contribute to theory, knowledge accumulation or the next step of research.
- Sentence/paragraph skeleton: `This has clear implications for...` / `The approach could be applied to...` / `The data provide evidence-based guidance for...`
- Completion criterion: The application's target, mode of use and conditions are clear, and do not exceed the research design.
- Source: Unit 4 §4.2.2, §4.4.2.

## 4. Discussion paragraph blueprint

1. Contribution entry: achievement, value, or reader reboot;
2. Key results: interpret the most important findings;
3. Research map: compare with, support, improve or challenge the literature;
4. Mechanism/theory: propose evidence-supported interpretations, retaining necessary modality;
5. Limitations: state the boundaries, their impact and possible alternative explanations;
6. Future directions: turn limitations into specific research;
7. Close: contribution, application, impact or next step.

The order is reasonably fluid; the achievement may recur at the start, in the middle and at the end, but each time it should carry a new argumentative function.

## 5. Discussion completion check

- Does the opening immediately show the paper's achievement or reboot the key question?
- Are key results interpreted rather than simply repeated?
- Is each interpretation adjacent to the evidence and to existing knowledge?
- Are the points of agreement, difference or advance with existing research made explicit?
- Are the internal achievement and the outward contribution kept distinct?
- Do the modal verbs and tenses match the strength of the evidence?
- Do the limitations lead to specific rather than vague future directions?
- Are the applications or impact consistent with the Introduction, Abstract and Conclusion?


## Worked examples from the book: how the Discussion moves forward after repetition

### Counter-example: merely repeating the Results

> In conclusion, pressure increased the rate by 23% under condition Y.

### Good example: restating, then giving the meaning

> The present study showed that pressure increased the rate by 23% under condition Y. This finding indicates that pressure-sensitive control may be relevant to applications in which condition Y is unavoidable.

The Discussion may repeat key results, but each repetition must move forward to significance, a literature comparison, a limitation or an application. When checking a paragraph, mark the new function added after each result-restatement sentence; if no new function is added, the paragraph may still be stuck in the Results.

### Counter-example and good example: novelty claims

> This is the first study ever to compare X and Y.

> To our knowledge, this is the first study to compare X and Y under condition Z.

`to our knowledge` confines "the first" to the scope of the author's search and qualifying conditions. For first, largest, only, always ask: what is the comparison range, and does it truly exclude relevant research?

### Counter-example and good example: commitment strength of future directions

> Future work will examine whether the same pattern occurs in larger populations.

> Future work should examine whether the same pattern occurs in larger populations.

`will` implies the author has personally committed to this work; `should` is a recommendation for subsequent research. Unless the author genuinely has a plan, `should` is more accurate.

### Counter-example and good example: mechanism inference

> The discrepancy must have been caused by differences in sample preparation.

> The discrepancy may have been caused by differences in sample preparation.

`must` signals that other explanations are virtually ruled out; `may` preserves uncertainty. The Discussion is not the place to write the most likely explanation as if it were certain.

Source: Unit 4, 4.2.2 and 4.5.
