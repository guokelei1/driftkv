# 08 Title and Keywords: The entry point and discovery tool of the research

## 1. The task of the Title

The Title is the part seen, searched and scanned by the most people. It should attract the right readers and predict what they will gain or what value they will get from reading the paper, rather than recording the working title in the researcher's head. The final title should be decided after the research is complete, the results analysed and compared with the literature.

**Systems-conference variant (governs this project; see guideline 14, SYS-11).** The dominant convention is `SystemName: What It Does For Whom`, e.g. "SystemName: Mechanism for Target Workload". A short, memorable system name becomes the paper's handle in reviews and citations; the post-colon part carries the object, action and target workload. All rules below (noun-chain control, preposition ambiguity, promise fulfilment) apply to the post-colon part. Systems venues generally do not use keyword lists; TITLE-03 applies only to journal submissions.

## 2. The seven-step model

### TITLE-01 Check the average length in the target journal

- Rule strength: NORMALLY.
- Writing action: Count the word count, syntax and colon use of recent comparable articles; treat the average as a starting point, not a hard ceiling.
- Source: Unit 7 §7.1.

### TITLE-02 Use acronyms with caution

- Rule strength: CONDITIONAL.
- Acronyms shared within a microcommunity may not be transparent to interdisciplinary and future readers; unless the target readership will almost certainly know them, write the full form or leave the acronym to the keyword list.
- Source: Unit 7 §7.2.

### TITLE-03 Make the title keywords and the keyword list complementary

- Rule strength: NORMALLY.
- The title is for quickly predicting the core result, method or object; the keyword list is for broadening the indexing range and attracting specialist readers. Do not mechanically repeat every word from the title.
- Source: Unit 7 §7.3.

### TITLE-04 Control compound noun chains and preposition ambiguity

- Rule strength: MUST.
- **Compound noun chains**: In a compound noun chain, only the rightmost noun is the "real" noun grammatically; the nouns to its left all function as adjectives modifying it. The longer the chain, the harder the modification is to unravel (e.g. oil can -> oil can opener -> ...). Control the length of compound noun chains.
- **Preposition overload**: Too many prepositions in the title make it impossible for the reader to judge the scope, purpose, method and object. Replace prepositions with content-bearing verbs: `interactions in gene transcription` -> `interactions that control gene transcription`.
- Fix action: Divert items to the keyword list; replace prepositions with content-bearing verbs or clear phrases; if necessary, split the title into a colon structure of general topic + contribution.
- Example: `[general topic]: a [value-bearing contribution] for [application]`.
- **Position ambiguity of `for the first time`**: In `X-ray imaging was used to study Y for the first time`, `for the first time` may modify "using X-ray imaging" or "studying Y". Place it next to the element it modifies, or rewrite as `For the first time, X-ray imaging was used to study Y`.
- Completion criterion: The target reader can quickly answer "what is the research object, what was done, and what was obtained/what is it for."
- Source: Unit 7 §7.4–7.5; Unit 8 §8.3.

### TITLE-04b Model the structural content from target titles

- Rule strength: NORMALLY.
- Check the structural patterns of recent titles in the target journal:
  - Do they begin with A/An (communicating a new offer)?
  - Do they use a colon (separating the general topic from the focus, or a new term from its definition)?
  - Do they include the method name?
  - Do they include the purpose or application?
  - Do they include "happy words" (accurate, robust, novel, efficient, etc.)?
  - Do they include acronyms?
  - Are they aimed at a specific microcommunity?
- Sentence titles: Some journals accept full sentence titles with subject + verb + object (Present Simple), especially when the findings are strong and clear. Check whether the target journal uses such titles. Modal verbs (may) may be used for implications not yet established.
- Source: Unit 7 §7.5.

### TITLE-05 Rewrite the research activity as an outcome or value

- Rule strength: NORMALLY.
- Common errors: `Investigating...`, `A study of...` only say that research was done; they do not say what the reader gains.
- Fix action: Turn the research activity into a method, model, finding, contribution, application or verifiable outcome.
- Sentence/paragraph skeleton types:
  - New method: `A [accurate/robust] method for [task]`;
  - Mechanism/result: `[Factor] enhances [outcome]`;
  - Topic + contribution: `[Topic]: a [new approach/analysis] for [purpose]`.
- Source: Unit 7 opening, §7.5.

### TITLE-06 Title promises must be fulfilled in the paper

- Rule strength: MUST.
- Writing action: Predict reader expectations from the title, then check whether the Abstract, Results and Conclusion actually deliver that content; if the evidence has not yet proven it, use may/might/could to indicate a directional possibility.
- Source: Unit 7 §7.6.

## 3. Title completion check

- Does the title come from the real outcome after the research was completed, rather than the original working title?
- Does it contain the most necessary of the central object, action/result, method or value?
- Are the relationships between nouns and prepositions parseable?
- Are acronyms transparent to target and future readers?
- Do the keywords extend rather than simply copy the title?
- Are the "new, accurate, fast, applied" implications hinted at by the title fulfilled in the body?
