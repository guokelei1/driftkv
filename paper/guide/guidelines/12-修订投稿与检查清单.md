# 12 Revision, Submission and Checklists

## 1. Revise from the top down to the bottom

### Layer 1: Research purpose and destination

- What is the central claim?
- Are achievement, contribution and impact prioritised?
- Do the Title, Abstract and Conclusion deliver the same take-home message?

### Layer 2: Section interfaces

- Does the gap in the Introduction lead to the design of the Methods?
- Does every research question/hypothesis have evidence in the Results?
- Are the findings in the Results interpreted in the Discussion?
- Are the contributions in the Discussion summarised with evidence constraints in the Conclusion?
- Is the scope of the research kept consistent within the limitations?

### Layer 3: Paragraphs

- Does each paragraph have only one central function?
- Does the entry sentence give the reader direction?
- Does each paragraph move naturally from the new information of the previous paragraph into the next?
- Are there loose sentences left over from notes?

### Layer 4: Sentences

- What is each sentence doing here?
- Are claim and evidence adjacent?
- Does known information come first?
- Does the connector express a real relation?
- Are pronouns, which, this and it clear?

### Layer 5: Language and format

- Are tense, voice and ownership accurate?
- Do causal, modal and evaluative words match the evidence?
- Are terminology, abbreviations, articles, prepositions and units consistent?
- Do sentence length, noun strings and prepositional strings increase processing burden?
- Are figures, citations, headings, supplementary materials and author guidelines complete?

## 2. Pre-submission master checklist

### Whole-text organisation

- [ ] Target reader and genre are defined.
- [ ] Sections, subheadings, paragraph length and figure habits have been calibrated against recent target articles.
- [ ] The whole article has a single central contribution that can be retold.
- [ ] Each section has a clear mission and boundary.
- [ ] The order in which the experiments happened has not been mistaken for the reader's best order.

### Introduction

- [ ] The shared starting point suits the target reader.
- [ ] Background moves from general to specific, with no technical jumps.
- [ ] Literature is organised by research development, not as a list.
- [ ] The gap/problem is specific, respectful and addressable by this paper.
- [ ] Aims/hypotheses correspond one-to-one with the gap.
- [ ] The preview of this paper's method, results and contribution is consistent with the main text.

### Methods

- [ ] There is an overview before the detail.
- [ ] Steps, parameters, equipment, materials, samples and timeline are sufficient for reproduction.
- [ ] Rationale for choices, quality control and comparison relations are clear.
- [ ] Ownership of this paper's work, others' work and standard procedures is unambiguous.
- [ ] Problems are stated where they occur, with their impact limited.

### Results

- [ ] Results are organised by research question and trend.
- [ ] The reader knows what to look at in each figure.
- [ ] Key results receive evaluation; secondary results do not steal the centre.
- [ ] The object and dimension of comparison are clear.
- [ ] Direct interpretation, speculation and application are not mixed into one strength.
- [ ] Anomalies, limitations and uninvestigated factors are appropriately stated.

### Discussion/Conclusion

- [ ] The Discussion opens or early on delivers the achievement/contribution.
- [ ] Key results are interpreted and put back into the research map.
- [ ] Achievement, contribution and impact are separate yet connected.
- [ ] Modal verbs are consistent with evidence strength.
- [ ] Limitations lead to specific future work.
- [ ] The Conclusion clearly attributes this paper's work and delivers the take-home message.

### Abstract/Title

- [ ] The Abstract can be read independently.
- [ ] The Abstract prioritises the central achievement, not filling the word count.
- [ ] The Title predicts the research output, not the work activity.
- [ ] Keywords complement the title.
- [ ] Noun strings, prepositions and abbreviations are unambiguous.
- [ ] The promise of the title is fulfilled in the main text.

### Systems papers (additional; see guideline 14)

- [ ] Section skeleton matches SYS-01: Intro → Motivation → Overview → Design ×2–3 → Implementation → Evaluation → Related Work → Conclusion.
- [ ] Introduction ends with 3–5 verifiable contribution bullets and a numeric results preview.
- [ ] Motivation contains the authors' own measurements and ends in numbered requirements answered by the design sections.
- [ ] The Overview defines abstractions, component responsibilities/interfaces, and a life-of-a-job walkthrough.
- [ ] The Evaluation is RQ-driven, opens each RQ with its answer, and includes baselines, ablations, scaling and overheads.
- [ ] Every speedup names baseline, tuning status, endpoint and residency; negative results are reported.
- [ ] Related Work is near the end, organized by comparison axis, citing measured comparisons where available.
- [ ] Most figures are data figures; the claim→evidence matrix and artifact plan are current.

## 3. Problem-to-action revision table

| Problem | Why it is a problem | Revision action |
|---|---|---|
| Information piled up but the reader does not know its use | The information has no narrative function | Tag each sentence with a function; add evaluation/relation or delete |
| The research map reads like a shopping list | Literature does not lead to this paper's motivation | Organise by general-to-specific, method or time, and add links |
| Results read like a figure catalogue | Data are not interpreted by the author | Give the overall trend first, then the figure, then key evaluation and implication |
| The Discussion only repeats Results | It does not move from finding to meaning | After each revisit of a result, add interpretation, comparison, boundary or application |
| Methods read like lab notes | Written for the author, not the reader | Add overview, rationale for choices, parameters, order and reproduction information |
| Passive sentences hide who did it | Ownership is lost | Add in this study/in their work or move to the active |
| Sentences are full of prepositions and nouns | Main-clause relations are obscured | Turn prepositions into verbs, split the sentence, make time/purpose/condition explicit |
| Terminology keeps changing name | The concept boundary is unstable | Choose one accurate term and repeat it |
| The conclusion is too strong | It exceeds the design or evidence | Weaken causal verbs, add modals/scope, or add evidence |
| Naked numbers have no evaluative direction | The reader judges high/low independently | Add evaluative words such as only/as high as/substantial |
| `must` is used for an inference that has evidence | must implies a lack of empirical evidence | When evidence is sufficient, state it directly; do not use must |
| `can` is ambiguous | It may mean possible/able/sometimes | Replace with it is possible/is able to/sometimes |
| A sentence begins with -ing or a preposition | Sentence start-up is unclear | Change to an explicit clause or a verb opener |
| The Discussion changes vocabulary on revisit | The echo breaks and the reader must re-judge | Repeat the exact words from the Introduction/Results |
| The implication is over-weakened | It undervalues the research | If the evidence supports it, upgrade the language strength |

## 4. Length and size benchmarks

The figures below come from the book's observation of more than 2,500 recent **journal** research articles; they are a starting point for journal submissions only.

| Part | Typical range | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Title | about 12 words | Average the target journal as a starting point |
| Abstract | 80–250 words, single paragraph | The word limit is a boundary, not a target |
| Abstract sentence length | about 22 words average | <20 words: ~90% of readers understand at first reading; >40 words: ~10% |
| Conclusion | 100–200 words, 1–2 paragraphs | Journal norm only; does not bind systems papers |
| Paragraph | about 150–170 words | Avoid whole-page paragraphs and consecutive single-sentence paragraphs |
| Sentence | about 20–26 words | Over 40 words more likely to require re-reading |
| Target articles | no fewer than 4 | Within five years, close to the topic, from the target venue |

For systems-conference papers, budget by page instead (guideline 14, SYS-10): a 12-page double-column body typically spends Intro 1–1.5, Motivation 1–1.5, Overview 1, Design 3–4, Implementation 0.5, Evaluation 4–5, Related 0.75, Conclusion 0.25 pages, with 8–12 mostly-data figures. The Evaluation must be the largest section. Abstract length follows the venue limit (typically 150–250 words).

## 5. Self-edit: clear out unconscious habits

In the final proofreading round, check these habitual forms specifically:

- **Lexical tics**: do `indeed`, `in fact`, `basically`, `clearly` actually add information, or are they just verbal habits? Does the meaning change if they are deleted?
- **Pairs of dashes**: the meaning of the content between dashes is not obvious, especially to international readers; translate the dash content into explicit language or make it a separate sentence.
- **Parentheses**: if the content is important enough to include, perhaps it should not be in parentheses; if it is not important enough, perhaps it should not be there at all.
- **Comma stacking**: using commas to keep adding information creates a rambling, over-long sentence; break it into shorter sentences with explicit links.
- **Passive verb at the end of a long sentence**: the reader has to wait a long time to find out what happened; bring the important verb forward or split the sentence.
- **The "magnetic south" check**: does every sentence and paragraph point towards the central destination of the paper? If a sentence does not serve the central argument, delete or relocate it.


## Second-pass check with counter-examples

After the structural and language checks, do not only ask "are there grammar errors". Open [Counter-examples and diagnostic cases from the original book](13-原书正反例与诊断案例.md), and use the corresponding cases within each section of the main text. For each key issue, record:

1. The minimal difference between the original sentence and the contrast sentence.
2. Whether the difference changes time, ownership, evidence strength, scope of modification or paragraph function.
3. The specific misreading the reader might produce.
4. The minimal fix that does not change the scientific fact.
5. A check question that can be reused in the next round.

Prioritise spot-checks of the gap in the Introduction, the step conditions in Methods, the causal wording in Results, the mechanism explanation in Discussion, and the contribution claims in the Abstract/Conclusion. The role of the book's cases is not to provide English that can be mechanically applied, but to help the author judge whether a sentence delivers their scientific intention accurately to the reader.
