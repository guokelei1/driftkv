# 06 Conclusion: Delivering the independently memorable take-home message

## 1. Section mission

The Conclusion may be a separate one or two short paragraphs, or the last part of the Discussion. It overlaps with the Abstract, Introduction and Discussion, yet it is not equivalent to repetition, because it compresses the study's outcome, value, boundaries and impact into a final message — the magnetic south towards which the whole text leads.

Length: the book's journal observation is 100–200 words; **for systems-conference papers this is not a norm** — one short paragraph to about a third of a page is typical, containing what was built, the key insight, the headline numbers and the take-home message. Limitations normally live in the Evaluation's discussion subsection, not here (guideline 14 SYS-01).

**Do not use the Abstract as the primary source material for the Conclusion.** The Abstract and the Conclusion serve different readers, appear in different positions and carry different functions. The Conclusion should be distilled from the body of the paper (especially the Results and Discussion), not copied from the Abstract.

## 2. The eleven optional components

```text
C1  What is in the paper
C2  What the paper/study has achieved
C3  Relevant background information
C4  The gap/aim/need for the study
C5  The method/approach
C6  Key results with evaluative comments
C7  Implications of the results
C8  Potential or actual limitations
C9  Potential or actual applications
C10 How the study advances knowledge
C11 Potential future directions for research
```

Not every Conclusion uses all eleven components; select and order them according to the target journal and the value of the research.

## 3. Rules

### CONC-01 Make ownership of the paper's contribution explicit

- Rule strength: MUST.
- Writing action: Use `we have...`, `this study...`, `in the present paper...`, Past Simple/Present Perfect, or an explicit `from the results of this study...`.
- Why: Readers may jump directly to the Conclusion; a non-human (agentless passive) subject in the Present Simple may look like established background fact or another researcher's contribution.
- Common errors: `Kinetic analyses suggest...`, `It is argued that...` without ownership can blur the study's own contribution with existing research.
- Completion criterion: Every major achievement can be unambiguously attributed to this study by the reader.
- Source: Unit 5 §5.4.2; Unit 8 §8.3.

### CONC-02 Turn key results into an outcome, not a copy of the full Results

- Rule strength: NORMALLY.
- Writing action: Select the results that best support the central contribution, add evaluation and significance; delete details unrelated to the take-home message.
- Sentence/paragraph skeleton: `We [achieved]. [Key result] was observed, which [meaning]. This [contribution/application].`
- Completion criterion: Without reading the body, the reader can know the study's results and value, without being distracted by all the secondary results.
- Source: Unit 5 §5.1–5.3.

### CONC-03 Use the appropriate tense to express status and completion

- Present Simple/Present Perfect: what is in the paper, currently valid achievements, implications and contributions;
- Past Simple: specific methods performed, key historical results or observed events;
- A strong conclusion still requires evidence and appropriate hedging; tense is not a means of exaggeration.
- Completion criterion: Each tense choice can be explained as either "what the research did in the past" or "what it is now taken to mean."
- Source: Unit 5 §5.4.1.

### CONC-04 Define the boundaries and make future directions actionable

- Rule strength: CONDITIONAL.
- Writing action: Include only the key limitations that affect the take-home message; turn limitations into follow-up research with a clear object, action and purpose.
- Applications: State who could use it and how; avoid presenting a possibility as a verified real-world impact.
- Completion criterion: The ending leaves not "more research is needed" but a reasonable, traceable next step.
- Source: Unit 4 §4.4.2; Unit 5 §5.2–5.4.

## 5. Conclusion paragraph template

### Short conclusion

1. `This study has [achievement].`
2. `Using [approach], we found [key result + evaluation].`
3. `These findings [contribution/implication], within [boundary].`
4. `Future work should [specific direction] / The approach could be applied to [qualified application].`

### Two-paragraph conclusion

- First paragraph: what the study did, the key results and the achievement;
- Second paragraph: contribution, application, limitations and future research.

## 6. Conclusion completion check

- Without reading the body, can the reader identify what this study did and achieved?
- Does the achievement use the same core verb as the Introduction aim?
- Do the key results carry evaluation, rather than bare numbers?
- Does the contribution go beyond "we did experiments"?
- Are applications and future directions expressed as evidence-supported conditionals?
- Have you avoided introducing a fatal limitation for the first time in the final paragraph?
- Is the central message consistent across the Title, Abstract, Discussion and Conclusion?


## Worked examples from the book: ownership and evidence strength in the Conclusion

### Counter-example: placing the analytical tool in the subject position

> Kinetic analyses suggest that the reaction proceeds through pathway X.

### Good example: making the authors' synthesising contribution explicit

> We have synthesized the data and kinetic analyses to show that the reaction proceeds through pathway X.

or:

> This study demonstrates that the reaction proceeds through pathway X.

The first sentence is not wrong, but the reader may not know whether the analytical tool automatically produced the conclusion or whether the authors synthesised the data to propose it. The good example makes the paper's contribution explicit; however, `demonstrates` requires stronger evidence than `suggest`.

### Positive vs negative structure of a conclusion

A full Conclusion can be compressed as needed into:

> This study examined X to address the unresolved question of Y. Using method Z, we found A. This finding contributes B, although the conclusion is limited by C. Future work should test D.

A logic-deficient counter-example:

> X was studied. Some results were obtained. More research is needed.

The latter does not recall the gap, method, key finding, contribution or limitation. The Conclusion should not merely repeat the last few sentences of the Abstract; it should let the reader leave the paper knowing "what state of knowledge this paper has changed."

### Check questions

- Does the conclusion explicitly respond to the Introduction gap?
- Is there a single sentence that states the paper's knowledge increment?
- Are the evidence strengths of suggest, show and demonstrate kept distinct?
- Does the limitation change the scope within which the conclusion applies?
- Does the future work arise naturally from the limitation or the unresolved mechanism?

Source: Unit 5, especially 5.4.2.
