# 11 Figures, Citations, Supplementary Materials and Structural Conventions

## 1. Figures are not decoration

### FIG-01 Every figure must have a narrative reason

- Rule strength: MUST.
- Writing action: explain why the figure is needed, the order in which it appears, what the reader should attend to, and how it supports the next result or conclusion.
- Location: it appears just when the reader feels the need for evidence, not randomly dumped at the start or end of Results.
- Completion criterion: if the figure is deleted, you can state exactly which observation, comparison or argument loses support.
- Book source: Planning in the Introduction; Unit 3 §3.2.2.

### FIG-02 Give the interpretive frame first, then let the reader see a potentially ambiguous figure

- Rule strength: CONDITIONAL.
- If the visual data are clear and unambiguous, the figure may come first; if the figure can sustain more than one interpretation, use text first to establish the dimension of observation, then invite the reader to look.
- `Figure 1 shows...` states what is in the figure; `As can be seen from Figure 1...` guides the inference.

### FIG-03 Systems-paper figure economy (external rule; see guideline 14, SYS-10)

- Rule strength: NORMALLY.
- A strong systems paper carries 8–12 figures/tables, and **most must be data figures** (bar, line, CDF, Pareto frontier, scaling curves), not architecture or flow diagrams. One or two architecture figures suffice; every headline claim deserves a data figure the reviewer can check.
- Tables are preferred for exact configuration, comparison matrices against prior systems, and claim-to-evidence maps; figures are preferred for trends, trade-offs and scaling.
- Convert tables into figures when the message is a trend or a frontier; a Pareto plot of cost vs fidelity communicates more than the table it summarizes.
- Completion criterion: the Evaluation contains more data figures than the rest of the paper contains diagrams; each contribution bullet can point to at least one figure/table.

## 2. Citation location and function

- A citation must immediately follow the fact, method, result or explanation it supports.
- When citing a standard method in Methods, still give the minimum steps the reader needs to understand this paper; do not force the reader off to look up the literature.
- In the Introduction, literature is selected to build the research map and motivation; in Results it is used to compare results; in Discussion it locates and explains the contribution. The same citation may have a different function in different locations.
- Stacking citations at the end of a sentence creates ambiguity of ownership, and may even credit your own work to others.
- The Abstract normally carries no citation, unless the article directly extends, substantially advances or refutes a specific work.

## 3. Supplementary materials and the main text

Detailed Methods content may go into Supplementary Materials, but the main text should still contain the information needed to make the Results and Conclusion understandable and to let the reader judge reliability. A large body of supplementary material without specific hyperlinks and locating pointers in the main text increases the burden of retrieval, understanding and peer review.

Check:

- Is the main text sufficient to understand the research logic?
- Are supplementary materials pointed to by specific section or item, rather than a generic "see Supplementary Materials"?
- Can the reader tell which method is central to this paper and which is only a standard procedure?

## 4. Subheadings and subsections

- A subheading should accurately represent the whole subsection; the subsection content should not gradually drift away from the heading.
- Use the keywords of the heading as early as possible in the subsection, so the reader builds an expectation.
- The type of study, the length and the target journal determine the number of subsections, the average length and whether sentence-style headings are used.
- The aim of structure is to help the reader locate function, not to create more levels.

## 5. The boundary of normative content

The focus of this book is content, narrative, language, citation and readability. Ethics, conflicts of interest, data sharing, reporting standards and specific submission formats should be checked separately against the target journal or disciplinary norms, and are not disguised as rules of the original book in this distillation. Where applicable, they can be added to the submission checklist as "external requirements of the target journal".
