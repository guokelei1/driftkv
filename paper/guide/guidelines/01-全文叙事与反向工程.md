# 01 Whole-Text Narrative and Reverse Engineering

## 1. Core Rules

### NAR-01 Write Information for the Reader, Not for the Research Group

- Location: Whole text.
- Rule strength: MUST.
- Writing goal: Let the reader know the function, basis and destination of every item of information.
- Writing action: After writing each piece of data, background or method, ask: "What is it here to make the reader know, believe or prepare for?"
- Why: The writer has long been familiar with the project and easily mistakes shared knowledge for reader knowledge; the reader does not know why, how, or how this item relates to the next. As the book stresses: The aim is not simply to make it possible for the reader to understand; the aim is to make it impossible for the reader NOT to understand.
- Common errors: Turning lab notes, information dumps or shorthand used between colleagues directly into the paper.
- Fix action: Add a function tag to the information; if the tag cannot be stated clearly, delete, move or supplement background and linkage.
- Completion criterion: Pick a sentence at random and the author can explain which part of the argument it serves; the reader need not guess why it appears.
- Source: Introduction; Unit 8 §8.1–8.2.

### NAR-02 Build a Narrative Wrap, Not Just Add Language Polishing

- Location: Titles, subtitles, graphic positions, paragraphs and sentences.
- Rule strength: MUST.
- Writing goal: Turn neutral science information/data into directional, readable text.
- Writing action: Plan genre/readership, subsections, graphic order, paragraph function and sentence function; then realise the scaffold while writing through evaluation, tense, sentence beginnings and terminology.
- Recommended order: Article map -> paragraph map -> sentence roles -> word/sentence surface form.
- Sentence/paragraph skeleton: `[known/background] + [action of this sentence] + [preparation for or result of what follows]`.
- Why: Information itself has no self-evident function; the narrative determines how the reader enters, processes and leaves the article.
- Completion criterion: Looking only at the title, subtitles and the first sentence of each paragraph, you can map the direction of the paper; looking only at the graphics and the surrounding narrative, you can understand why they are there.
- Source: The two narrative wrap diagrams in the Introduction and the Planning/Writing subsections.

### NAR-03 Identify Genre and Readership First, Then Decide Content Density

- Location: The starting point of writing planning.
- Rule strength: NORMALLY.
- Writing action: Clarify whether you are writing a research article, review, grant proposal, Letter, poster, conference abstract, thesis or report; collect comparable target texts and observe the normal sections, length, headings and information density.
- Conditions and variants: When migrating across genres you cannot simply cut-and-paste; when resubmitting to a different journal you need to re-estimate the reader.
- Common errors: Pasting the Introduction of a paper directly into an abstract, a grant application or a poster.
- Completion criterion: You can answer "Who are the readers of this paper, what can be assumed, how soon can you move into technical detail, and which items of information need to appear independently".
- Source: Introduction's Planning: Identifying Genre and Readership; Unit 1 §1.2.2; Unit 6 §6.1.

### NAR-04 Reverse Engineer Recent Successful Papers, Don't Memorise Fixed Templates

- Location: All sections.
- Rule strength: MUST.
- Writing action: From at least four papers from the past five years, close to your own topic and from your target venue or similar venues (for this project: recent systems-conference papers such as those accepted at SOSP/OSDI/NSDI/ATC/EuroSys), record separately the title, abstract, subtitles, graphics, the first sentence of each paragraph and the sentence functions. The stable conventions already extracted from such papers are recorded in [14-系统论文结构与体裁.md](14-系统论文结构与体裁.md); reverse engineering serves to re-calibrate them per submission, not to rediscover them.
- Recommended action: First ask "what is this sentence doing", not "what is this sentence saying"; delete the discipline content words from the model, keeping only the transferable functions.
- Typical record: `Sentence 1 establishes significance`, `Sentence 2 supplies background fact`, `Sentence 3 identifies gap`.
- Why: Writing conventions change fast, and a descriptive model adapts to new platforms, new journals and new article types better than a "should always" prescription. The approach is descriptive rather than prescriptive, and applies across all STEMM (Science, Technology, Engineering, Mathematics and Medicine) disciplines and text types.
- Common errors: Collecting only pretty sentences; treating the three-paragraph structure of a single example as the only structure for all papers.
- Completion criterion: The model still explains most sentences when applied to a different target article, and it retains anomalies and conditions.
- Source: Preface to Second Edition; Introduction's reverse-engineering strategy; Units 1–6 Exercises.

### NAR-05 List Achievement, Contribution, Impact First, Then Plan the Whole Text

- Location: After the research is complete, before writing the title and body.
- Rule strength: NORMALLY.
- Writing action: List in order of importance: what was accomplished internally in the research; what knowledge or method was added; what possible impact on the research line or on reality; select one central take-home message.
- Why: If there is no destination, the Results and Discussion tend to diffuse, and the Title, Abstract and Conclusion will each stress different things.
- Completion criterion: You can state the central value in one sentence; the Title, Abstract, Discussion and Conclusion use the same core concept and appropriate strength.
- Source: Unit 4 §4.1.1; Unit 8 §8.1.

## 2. Reverse Engineering Workflow

1. **Build a text map**: title, Abstract, section headings, graphics and conclusion.
2. **Build a paragraph map**: the first sentence of each paragraph, paragraph function, transitions between paragraphs.
3. **Build a sentence function table**: tag each sentence as background, claim, evidence, comparison, explanation, limitation or transition.
4. **Mine language**: collect only the verbs, connectors, evaluative words and modal words that achieve the functions.
5. **Check grammatical meaning**: observe how tense, passive/active, pronouns, prepositions and articles change ownership or strength.
6. **Write your own version**: generate text using variable slots, without copying discipline nouns and surface sentence patterns.
7. **Read back and update**: add new structures found in new target articles to the model; delete outdated absolute rules.

## 3. Whole-Text Storyline Checklist

- Does the research question emerge naturally from the background and the gap?
- Do the design and implementation actually answer the problem stated in the Introduction?
- Is the evidence ordered by argumentative need rather than by the order in which experiments occurred?
- Does the interpretation move beyond the results, rather than merely repeat them?
- Does the Conclusion deliver a central message consistent with the title and abstract?
- Does every strong word such as "first", "best", "effective", "accurate" have evidence and a scope?
- Do graphics, citations and supplementary materials appear at the point when the reader needs them?
- For a systems paper: do the contribution bullets, design sections and evaluation research questions form a one-to-one chain (see guideline 14)?


## Worked Examples from the Book: How the Same Information Becomes Readable Narrative

### Counter-example: Just laying out the information

> Smith et al. studied X. Jones et al. studied Y. Lee et al. studied Z.

### Good example: Organising the research history into a reader path

> A pioneering study demonstrated X. Subsequent studies extended this approach to Y. However, whether the same mechanism applies to Z remains unclear.

The good example does not add many facts, but it adds three relationships: who is the starting point, how subsequent work extended it, and where the gap is. When checking a literature review paragraph, you must not only check whether the citations are complete, but also whether the reader can answer "why do these studies appear in this order".

### Counter-example: A result with no reader function

> The effect occurred in 23% of cases.

### Good example: Putting the number into the argument

> The effect occurred in as many as 23% of cases.

or:

> The effect occurred in only 23% of cases.

The three sentences have the same number, but the evaluative direction differs. Only when the baseline or expectation is clear do as many as and only have a basis. Part of the whole-text narrative check is to confirm that numbers, citations and terminology are all serving some reader judgement, rather than just "occupying a sentence".

### Counter-example: Announcing the gap with an absolute sentence

> No one has studied C.

### Good example: Qualifying the judgement you can actually support

> Although C has been considered in related systems, its effect under condition Z remains unclear.

The good example limits the gap to the system and the condition, avoiding overstating "the search found nothing" as "no one has studied it". This is also a point to observe closely when reverse engineering target articles: how excellent papers normally acknowledge existing work, and how they precisely preserve their own increment.

Source: Unit 1's information functions, research map, gap and whole-text narrative sections.
