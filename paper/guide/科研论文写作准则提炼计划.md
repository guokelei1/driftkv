# Science Research Writing Guidelines Extraction Plan

> **2026-07 amendment.** This plan describes the extraction of the original book only. After using the extracted rules on real manuscripts, the project found that the book's journal/IMRaD defaults do not match this project's actual genre (CS systems conference papers). The rule library was therefore extended with an explicitly marked external layer, [guidelines/14-系统论文结构与体裁.md](guidelines/14-系统论文结构与体裁.md), which governs structure/section naming/ordering/length for systems papers, while the book-derived rules remain authoritative for sentence-level language. This extension is compatible with §2.8 below because it is clearly marked as not coming from the book (see `traceability/规则来源索引.md`).

## 1. Project Goal

The goal of this project is not to compress or retell the book, but to transform it into a set of "operable guidelines" that can be directly used for writing, revising and checking science research papers.

The final product should enable the writer, without re-reading the whole book, to answer the following questions:

1. Which functional parts make up a research paper, and what task does each part perform?
2. What information should each part contain, and what should it not contain?
3. In what logical order should the paragraphs within each part be arranged?
4. What function does each sentence in each paragraph perform?
5. What logical relationships should be established between adjacent sentences, paragraphs and sections?
6. How should a sentence be expressed to be accurate, clear, concise and in line with science research writing conventions?
7. How can one judge that a part has been written completely and correctly?
8. Under different disciplines, research types or length constraints, which rules must be followed and which can be adjusted?

Therefore, the final product should be a "rule library + structural templates + checklists", not an "abridged textbook".

## 2. Core Principles of the Conversion Work

### 2.1 Convert from "knowledge content" to "writing actions"

The explanations, discussions, cases and experience in the book should be converted as far as possible into actions the writer can perform.

For example, do not just write:

> The Introduction should state the importance of the research.

Instead, convert it into:

- Goal: make the reader understand why this research question is worth solving.
- Location: normally completed in the front part of the Introduction.
- Writing action: first define the research object or field, then state its practical or theoretical importance.
- Inter-sentence logic: research background -> importance -> current limitation or unresolved problem.
- Completion criterion: before seeing the specific research method, the reader can already answer "why this research is needed".
- Common error: only saying "this problem is very important" without giving facts, impact or logical basis.

### 2.2 Organise by "rhetorical function", not only by book chapter

The book's chapters are the entry point for extracting information, but the final guidelines should be organised by the writer's actual tasks, for example:

- Establish the research background;
- Build the research importance;
- Point out the existing research gap;
- Pose the research question;
- Describe the research design;
- Report the results;
- Interpret the results;
- Bound the conclusion;
- Form paragraph and inter-sentence cohesion.

The same writing function may be scattered across multiple chapters of the book and must be merged; the same chapter may also contain multiple functions and must be split.

### 2.3 Retain four levels of granularity simultaneously

Each rule should cover the following four levels as far as possible:

1. **Whole-paper level**: the argumentation route and section relationships of the entire paper;
2. **Section level**: the goals and boundaries of each part such as Introduction, Methods, Results;
3. **Paragraph level**: the single function each paragraph performs, its internal order and inter-paragraph transitions;
4. **Sentence level**: the task of each sentence, its front-back relationships, common expression patterns and quality requirements.

You must not only summarise macro sections, nor reduce the product to scattered phrasing tips.

### 2.4 Distinguish rule strength

To prevent advice being wrongly written as absolute rules, each rule should be marked as:

- **MUST**: violation normally causes logic gaps, unclear facts, non-reproducible methods or academic misconduct issues;
- **NORMALLY**: applies to most research papers, but may be adjusted under explicit conditions;
- **OPTIONAL STRATEGY**: used to enhance persuasiveness, readability or textual effect;
- **CONDITIONAL**: applies only to specific research types, disciplines, journals or article structures.

### 2.5 Retain applicability conditions and exceptions

Any statement involving "the first sentence must..." "the Introduction must be three paragraphs" etc. must be verified as to whether it is:

- A universal rule;
- A common structure;
- A typical pattern of a certain type of paper;
- A single example the book uses to illustrate a point.

Unless the book explicitly gives a mandatory conclusion, the sentence count, paragraph count or order in a case must not be mechanically absolutised. The final guidelines may give a "recommended skeleton" but must state the adjustable conditions.

### 2.6 Examples serve rules, they do not replace them

When handling the book's examples, use the following conversion chain:

> Original example -> problem the example addresses -> the author's evaluation of the example -> generalisable principle -> applicability boundary -> short demonstration

The final product keeps only the shortest demonstration needed to understand the rule. Do not copy large numbers of the book's cases, and do not make the reader guess the rule from examples on their own.

### 2.7 Traceable, but not piled up with book notes

Each important rule should record its source location, such as the book's chapter, section or Markdown heading path. Source records are for verification and do not all need to be stuffed into the author-facing main text.

Two versions can ultimately be maintained:

- **Usage version**: concise, direct, oriented toward paper writing;
- **Traceability table**: records rule numbers, source locations, the book's argumentation and handling notes.

The book's content should mainly be summarised and paraphrased; avoid long verbatim copying.

### 2.8 Do not add unmarked external rules

The main basis in the extraction stage is this book. If external information is added to complete modern submission conventions, disciplinary conventions or other writing models, it must be separately marked as a "supplementary rule" with its source recorded, and must not be mixed with the book's views.

## 3. Overall Structure of the Final Guidelines

The final guidelines are suggested to be divided into six layers. They are not a simple replica of the book's chapters, but entry points for the writer's use when writing a paper.

### Layer 1: One-page core framework

For quickly recalling the task chain of the whole paper:

> Research field and background -> important problem -> existing work and gap -> research aim/question -> method and design -> evidence and results -> interpretation and comparison -> limitations and boundaries -> conclusion and contribution

This layer keeps only the most critical, cross-disciplinary and high-frequency rules.

### Layer 2: Whole-paper argumentation and storyline

Key extractions:

- What is the central claim of the paper;
- How the research question, method, results and conclusion correspond one to one;
- How to control the scope of the whole paper;
- How to arrange the order in which information appears;
- How sections connect to each other;
- How the title, abstract, main text and conclusion stay consistent;
- How to distinguish "research facts", "writer's interpretation" and "speculation".

### Layer 3: Component parts of the paper

Suggest at least the following modules:

1. Title, keywords and author information;
2. Abstract;
3. Introduction;
4. Background / Related Work / Literature Review;
5. Research Gap, Motivation, Research Question, Hypothesis and Contributions;
6. Materials and Methods / Methodology;
7. Research Design, Experiments and Evaluation;
8. Results;
9. Discussion;
10. Limitations, Validity and Reproducibility;
11. Conclusion;
12. Figures, Tables, Equations, Captions and appendices;
13. Citations, References, acknowledgements, ethics and conflict of interest and other normative content.

Some disciplines merge or split the above modules; the final guidelines should state the correspondence rather than forcing all papers to use the same headings.

### Layer 4: Paragraph and sentence logic

Key points:

- What kind of central task a paragraph should have;
- The roles of topic sentences, development sentences, evidence sentences, explanation sentences, limitation sentences and transition sentences;
- How given and new information are arranged;
- How claims and evidence are adjacent;
- How causal, contrastive, progressive, transitional, exemplifying, conditional and summarising relations are explicitly established;
- How reference objects are kept clear;
- How paragraphs move naturally from an old problem to a new one;
- What content should be split into sentences and what should be merged;
- How to avoid logic jumps, information repetition and isolated sentences.

### Layer 5: Science research language and expression

Key points:

- Accurate, concise, specific and unambiguous;
- Consistent use of terminology;
- Choice of verbs, nominalisation, active voice and passive voice;
- The function of tense in different sections;
- Modal verbs and cautious expression;
- Accurate statement of quantity, comparison, causality and significance;
- Negation, scope limitation and conditional expression;
- Avoiding vague judgements, exaggeration, ambiguous pronouns and unnecessary ornamentation;
- Reducing syntactic load without sacrificing accuracy.

### Layer 6: Revision, checking and submission

Key points:

- The layered revision order from structure, argumentation, paragraph, sentence to format;
- How to check whether the research question and conclusion close;
- How to check whether the method is sufficient to support the results;
- How to check whether the results support the claims;
- How to check whether graphics and main text complement each other;
- How to cut, seek peer feedback and do final proofreading;
- How to adjust format and length for the target journal or conference.

## 4. What Specifically to Extract for Each Part

For each of the above modules, use the same set of questions for extraction. This avoids uneven depth across different section summaries.

### 4.1 Section mission

- What problem does this part solve for the whole paper?
- After reading it, what should the reader know, believe or be able to judge?
- What is its relationship with the preceding and following parts?

### 4.2 Essential content

- What information is indispensable?
- What kind of evidence or detail does each item of information need?
- To what degree must the content be written to be sufficient?

### 4.3 Content boundaries

- What information belongs to this part?
- What content should be moved to other parts?
- What content is easily repeated?
- What content, if too much, will obscure the main line?

### 4.4 Recommended internal order

Extract separately:

- What functional units the part can be split into;
- The common precedence relationship of the functional units;
- The task of each paragraph;
- The task of each sentence;
- Conditions under which units can be omitted, merged or reordered.

### 4.5 Logical relationships

Explicitly mark the relationship between adjacent units, for example:

- Background -> focus;
- Known -> unknown;
- Current state -> gap;
- Problem -> research aim;
- Aim -> method;
- Method choice -> rationale for choice;
- Result -> evidence;
- Finding -> interpretation;
- This study -> existing research;
- Conclusion -> applicability boundary.

### 4.6 Language realisation

- What function common sentence patterns perform;
- What tense, voice or degree of caution should be used;
- Where in the sentence key words should be placed;
- How to turn abstract rules into expression frameworks that can be imitated but are not rigid.

Expression frameworks should use variable slots, for example:

> Existing studies have established **[known fact]**; however, **[unresolved problem]** remains unclear.

Templates should state the function and conditions of use, and must not encourage mechanical sentence-fitting.

### 4.7 Common errors and fixes

For each module, summarise at least:

- Missing-type errors;
- Order-type errors;
- Logic-type errors;
- Evidence-type errors;
- Expression-type errors;
- Boundary-crossing or repetition-type errors.

For each type of error, give "error manifestation -> why it is a problem -> fix action" as far as possible.

### 4.8 Completion check

For each module, form a set of yes/no check questions. Check items must be observable, avoiding inoperable statements like "well written" or "clear enough".

For example:

- Is a specific knowledge gap explicitly pointed out before the research aim is posed?
- Can every main research question find corresponding content in Methods, Results and Discussion?
- Does every causal conclusion have design and evidence sufficient to support a causal relationship?

## 5. Standard Format for a Single Rule

All rules use a unified template, suggested as follows:

```markdown
### [Rule number] Rule name

- Location:
- Rule strength: MUST / NORMALLY / OPTIONAL STRATEGY / CONDITIONAL
- Writing goal:
- Writing action:
- Recommended location or order:
- Relation to preceding text:
- Relation to following text:
- Sentence or paragraph skeleton:
- Why:
- Applicability conditions:
- Exceptions or variants:
- Common errors:
- Fix method:
- Completion criterion:
- Source location:
```

Not every rule needs to mechanically fill all fields, but at least the following must be present:

1. Location;
2. Writing goal;
3. Writing action;
4. Logical relationship or order;
5. Completion criterion;
6. Source location.

### Example: Converting a narrative into a rule

```markdown
### INTRO-GAP-01 Explicitly point out the specific unresolved problem

- Location: middle of the Introduction, after existing knowledge and before the research aim.
- Rule strength: NORMALLY.
- Writing goal: let the reader understand why existing research is still insufficient to answer this paper's question.
- Writing action: first summarise the known results directly relevant to this paper, then point out a clearly-scoped gap that this paper can address.
- Recommended order: existing knowledge -> specific limitation/unknown -> impact of the gap -> this paper's aim.
- Relation to preceding text: draws together the background information rather than abruptly switching topic.
- Relation to following text: the gap must naturally lead to this paper's research question, aim or hypothesis.
- Common error: claiming "research is scarce" without stating what is missing - object, data, mechanism, method or evidence.
- Completion criterion: the reader can restate in one sentence "what is currently unknown, and why this paper can address it".
- Source location: [to be filled when extracting]
```

This example only illustrates the final format; it does not prejudge that this is a specific rule in the book.

## 6. Decomposition and Processing Workflow for Large Markdown

## Stage 1: Build a book structure map

The goal is to know "what is in the book", not to immediately summarise paragraph by paragraph.

Operations:

1. Extract heading levels, section boundaries and the table of contents;
2. For each chapter, record the topic, length and the paper part it may involve;
3. Build a preliminary mapping of "book chapter -> target guideline module";
4. Mark cross-chapter topics, such as logic, syntax, graphics, revision and submission;
5. Identify case-dense areas, summary areas, rule-dense areas and exercise areas.

Products:

- Book structure table of contents;
- Target module mapping table;
- To-be-processed chunk list;
- List of possibly duplicated or cross-chapter topics.

Note: mechanically chunking by Markdown word count will fracture the context. Prioritise splitting by headings and argumentation units; for overly long chapters, split further by subsection, example or topic, and retain the heading path.

## Stage 2: Build work packages by target module

Do not simply adopt a final structure of "Chapter 1 summary, Chapter 2 summary". Build two parallel indexes:

- **Source index**: ensures every relevant part of the book is processed;
- **Topic index**: gathers rules of the same type scattered throughout the book.

Suggested work packages:

1. Whole-paper goal, readership and central argumentation;
2. Title, abstract and keywords;
3. Introduction and Motivation;
4. Literature, background, gap and contribution;
5. Research question, aim and hypothesis;
6. Methods, materials and procedure;
7. Research Design, Experiments and Evaluation;
8. Results and evidence presentation;
9. Discussion, interpretation and comparison;
10. Limitations, Validity and Reproducibility;
11. Conclusion;
12. Figures, tables, equations, captions and appendices;
13. Paragraph organisation and inter-sentence logic;
14. Words, syntax, tense, voice and tone;
15. Citations, ethics and norms;
16. Revision, proofreading and submission.

If the book uses different classifications, map the functions first, then decide to add, delete or merge work packages.

## Stage 3: Extract "candidate rules" chunk by chunk

Each chunk does not directly write the final summary; first build a candidate rule table. When reading, capture separately:

- Rules the author explicitly states;
- Rules confirmable from positive/negative example contrast;
- Reason explanations given for a certain way of writing;
- Recommended orders of sections, paragraphs and sentences;
- Applicability conditions and exceptions of rules;
- Common errors;
- Fix steps;
- Criteria usable for checking;
- Content that may duplicate or conflict with other rules.

Candidate rule record format:

```markdown
| Temp ID | Source path | Original text type | Preliminary rule | Location | Strength | Conditions/exceptions | To-verify issue |
```

Where "original text type" can be marked as: explicit rule, explanation, positive example, negative example, summary, exercise or inference.

Rules inferred only from examples must be marked "to verify" until supported by the author's explanation or other chapters.

## Stage 4: Atomise the candidate content

One candidate entry keeps only one main action. If one sentence contains multiple requirements at the same time, split it into multiple rules.

For example:

> The Introduction should briefly introduce the background, point out the problem and state this paper's contribution.

Should be split into at least three items:

1. Establish the background needed to understand the research question;
2. Narrow from the background to the specific problem or gap;
3. State how this paper responds to the problem and what contribution it produces.

Only after atomisation can order, applicability conditions and check items be accurately arranged.

## Stage 5: Merge, deduplicate and handle conflicts

Merge candidate rules on the same topic:

1. Merge semantically identical rules, retaining multiple sources;
2. Establish parent-child relationships between macro principles and specific operations, without replacing each other;
3. For different expressions in different chapters, extract their common core;
4. For seemingly contradictory rules, check whether they differ due to discipline, section function, research type or expression context;
5. Unresolvable conflicts should be recorded side by side with their conditions of use noted;
6. Strong conclusions supported by only a single example should be downgraded in strength or retained as strategies.

## Stage 6: Rebuild section, paragraph and sentence skeletons

This is the key stage that distinguishes this project from an ordinary reading summary.

For each paper part, form at least three representations:

### A. Functional sequence

For example:

> Establish context -> focus the problem -> review the current state -> identify the gap -> pose the aim -> preview the contribution

### B. Paragraph blueprint

Record:

- What problem the 1st functional paragraph solves;
- How the 2nd functional paragraph connects;
- Which functions can be done in the same paragraph;
- Under what conditions paragraphs need to be added, deleted or adjusted;
- What information each paragraph should start and end with.

Here "functional paragraph" is used rather than rigid "first paragraph, second paragraph, third paragraph". If the book explicitly recommends a fixed paragraph structure, retain it as a specific template.

### C. Sentence role table

Label a role for each type of sentence, for example:

- Background sentence;
- Scope-limiting sentence;
- Claim sentence;
- Evidence sentence;
- Explanation sentence;
- Contrast sentence;
- Gap sentence;
- Aim sentence;
- Method-overview sentence;
- Result-statement sentence;
- Limitation sentence;
- Transition sentence.

Then explain the common front-back connection relationships of each role. The final product should let the writer check sentence by sentence: "What task does this sentence perform here? If it is deleted, what is missing from the logic chain?"

## Stage 7: Write the guideline draft

Following the standard format in Section 5, write the merged rules into the target modules.

When writing, follow:

- Write the goal first, then the method;
- Write general rules first, then conditional variants;
- Write content and logic first, then surface language form;
- Rewrite "avoid a certain problem" into a positive fix action;
- Replace vague evaluation with observable completion criteria;
- Retain a source number for each important rule.

## Stage 8: Generate multiple usage views

The same rule library should generate usage versions at different granularities:

1. **Full-version guidelines**: retain explanations, conditions, errors and fix methods;
2. **Section writing templates**: guide drafting by functional order;
3. **Sentence-by-sentence checklist**: for checking sentence tasks and inter-sentence relationships;
4. **Pre-submission checklist**: keep only quickly confirmable items;
5. **Quick reference version**: kept within a short length for handy reference while writing;
6. **Traceability table**: for checking against the book and subsequent maintenance.

These views should come from the same rule numbering system to avoid contradictions between different versions.

## Stage 9: Quality verification

Verification is divided into four types.

### 9.1 Completeness verification

- Has every relevant chapter of the book entered the source index?
- Has every candidate rule been adopted, merged, downgraded or explicitly excluded?
- Do the main parts of the paper all have goal, content, order, language, errors and check items?
- Are cross-chapter topics missed?

### 9.2 Faithfulness verification

- Does the rule strength exceed the book's original intent?
- Are examples mistaken for universal rules?
- Is the extractor's experience mixed into the book's views?
- Are conditions and exceptions compressed away?
- Can rules be traced back to the corresponding source?

### 9.3 Consistency verification

- Does the same term keep the same meaning?
- Are the requirements for the same action consistent across different sections?
- Is the correspondence between Abstract, Introduction, Results, Discussion and Conclusion closed?
- Are the rules, templates and checklists consistent with each other?

### 9.4 Usability verification

Select at least one existing paper or a simulated text passage for trial use:

- Can the guidelines determine what to write next?
- Can the function and location of a sentence be judged?
- Can check items be used to find specific problems?
- Can discovered problems be turned into clear fix actions?
- Are there "correct but inoperable" vague rules?
- Can the quick reference version be efficiently retrieved during writing?

Problems found in trial use should go back to the rule library for revision, not just fix the examples.

## 8. Definition of Done for Each Work Package

A work package is only considered complete when all the following conditions are met:

1. All relevant locations in the book mapped to this topic have been covered;
2. Explicit rules and case-supported candidate rules have been extracted;
3. Rule atomisation, deduplication and strength marking are complete;
4. The goal, boundaries and essential content of the part have been stated;
5. An internal functional order has been formed;
6. A paragraph blueprint and main sentence roles have been formed;
7. The logical interface with preceding and following sections has been stated;
8. Conditions, exceptions and disciplinary variants have been recorded;
9. Common errors and fix actions have been summarised;
10. Observable completion check items have been generated;
11. All important rules can be traced back to a source;
12. At least one consistency and usability review has been passed.

## 9. Deviations to Especially Prevent

### 9.1 Writing chapter summaries

Chapter summaries answer "what the book says"; writing guidelines must answer "what the writer should do now, and how to know it is done right".

### 9.2 Extracting only macro structure

Section names like IMRaD are not enough. You must go deeper into paragraph functions, sentence roles and inter-sentence logic.

### 9.3 Collecting only sentence patterns

Sentence patterns must be attached to writing functions. A sentence-pattern library without applicability conditions and logical locations easily produces stiff, hollow papers.

### 9.4 Writing typical order as the only order

Prioritise describing functional dependencies, then give common arrangements. Only mark order as a strong requirement when one step logically depends on a previous step.

### 9.5 Deleting reasons for the sake of brevity

Guidelines can be much shorter than the book, but must not leave only commands. Retaining a concise "why" helps the writer adjust rules correctly in new situations.

### 9.6 Retaining too many cases for the sake of completeness

Each rule usually needs only one short positive example or one "problem-fix" contrast. The task of cases is to clarify rules, not to replicate the book's teaching process.

### 9.7 Confusing results, interpretation and conclusion

When extracting, specifically track how the book distinguishes:

- Observed or measured results;
- Interpretation of the results;
- Comparison with existing work;
- Conclusions supportable by evidence;
- Speculation beyond the evidence.

### 9.8 Ignoring cross-section correspondence

At least the following correspondence checks should be established:

| Front-end promise | Back-end response |
|---|---|
| Introduction problem or gap | How it is handled in Methods |
| Research Question / Hypothesis | Corresponding evidence in Results |
| Method choice | Validation in Evaluation |
| Result | Interpretation in Discussion |
| Claimed contribution | Evidence-constrained summary in Conclusion |
| Research scope | Boundaries in Limitations |

### 9.9 Ignoring different research types

Experimental studies, observational studies, theoretical studies, method papers, review papers and system papers may adopt different structures. The final guidelines should give general functions first, then conditional variants.

## 10. Recommended Execution Order

If formally starting the conversion, the following order is suggested:

1. Build the book's heading structure and topic map;
2. Design and fix the rule numbering, templates and glossary;
3. First process one chapter or one module as a pilot;
4. Use the pilot to test the extraction granularity and final format;
5. After revising the templates, batch-process the remaining work packages;
6. Prioritise completing the main-line modules: whole-paper logic, Introduction, Methods/Design, Results/Evaluation, Discussion and Conclusion;
7. Then process horizontal modules: graphics, language, citations, revision and submission;
8. Book-wide deduplication, conflict handling and terminology unification;
9. Rebuild paragraph blueprints, sentence roles and section interfaces;
10. Generate full version, template version, checklist and quick reference version;
11. Conduct coverage, faithfulness, consistency and usability verification;
12. Trial use with an actual paper and complete final revision.

The pilot stage is very important. Confirm "how granular rules should be" and "how to express sentence-level structure" before large-scale extraction, otherwise it is easy to rework everything after processing a large amount of content.

## 11. Acceptance Criteria for the Final Product

The final product should simultaneously meet the following standards:

### Content

- Covers the main writing tasks of science research papers from topic expression, structure organisation to revision and submission;
- Retains the book's core views, key conditions and important exceptions;
- Clarifies the functions, boundaries and connections of each section;
- Contains paragraph-level and sentence-level operational guidance;
- Provides sufficiently detailed functional sequences for key parts such as Introduction, Motivation, Design, Evaluation;
- Can distinguish structural variants across different research types and disciplines.

### Form

- Each rule is concise, atomised, with a stable number;
- Rule strength, location and source are clear;
- Templates, main text and checklists correspond to each other;
- Examples are short and clearly state the rule they demonstrate;
- Can be retrieved by section, writing task and error type.

### Usability

- The writer can build a paper structure from scratch following the guidelines;
- The writer can arrange the paragraphs and sentences of a section following the guidelines;
- The writer can use it to diagnose an existing draft;
- Every diagnostic result leads to a specific fix action;
- The quick version is concise enough, and the full version retains necessary explanations;
- No need to frequently return to the book to understand and use the core rules.

### Faithfulness

- Does not disguise the extractor's preferences as the book's views;
- Does not absolutise case structures;
- Does not lose key qualifying conditions due to compression;
- All important rules are traceable;
- External supplementary content is clearly separated from book-extracted content.

## 12. One-Sentence Working Principle of the Project

> Do not abridge by "what this book says"; reconstruct by "what function to accomplish when writing a paper, in what logic to accomplish it, how exactly to write it, and how to judge completion".
