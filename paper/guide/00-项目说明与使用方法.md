# Science Research Writing Guidelines: Project Description and Usage

## 1. What Problem Does This Documentation Set Solve

This is not a chapter-by-chapter summary of *Science Research Writing*; it turns the book into a set of tools that can be executed at the writing desk. Each rule tries to answer six questions:

1. What task must this part accomplish for the reader?
2. What should the writer write first, and what later?
3. What functions do paragraphs and sentences respectively perform?
4. How can this function be made clear in English?
5. What kinds of writing cause the reader to misunderstand, lose direction, or fail to see the writer's contribution?
6. How can observable check items be used to judge that the writing is in place?

The core principle is:

> Data and information do not automatically tell the reader why they are there; the writer must wrap the information in a reader-oriented narrative. The aim is not simply to make it possible for the reader to understand; the aim is to make it impossible for the reader NOT to understand.

## 1.1 Primary genre of this project: CS systems conference papers

The papers written in this project target CS systems conferences (SOSP/OSDI/NSDI/ATC/EuroSys/FAST and similar), not STEMM journals. The original book describes journal IMRaD conventions; several of its structural defaults (a "Methods" section, a separate "Discussion", 100–200-word Conclusions, journal abstracts) do NOT match the systems-conference genre and must not be followed literally.

Therefore:

- **Structure, section naming, ordering and length are governed by [guidelines/14-系统论文结构与体裁.md](guidelines/14-系统论文结构与体裁.md)** (Introduction → Background & Motivation → Overview → Design ×2–3 → Implementation → RQ-driven Evaluation → Related Work → Conclusion). Open it first for any whole-paper or section-structure task.
- The book-derived guidelines remain authoritative for the genre-independent layers: paragraph function, sentence linking, ownership, tense, modal traps, evaluative direction on numbers, echo language, figure narrative.
- Wherever a book-derived guideline says "target journal", read "target venue and its recent accepted papers".

## 2. Basis and Boundaries of the Documentation

- Source material: [b.md](../b.md), Hilary Glasman-Deal, *Science Research Writing*, Second Edition.
- Primary basis: the book's Introduction, Units 1–8, Appendix A–B, Writing Skills Index and Language Index.
- Project plan: [科研论文写作准则提炼计划.md](科研论文写作准则提炼计划.md).
- Rule strengths derive from the book's descriptive, reverse-engineering approach; the sentence count, paragraph count or order of any single example is not absolutised.
- Target journals, disciplines and article types may alter the specific structure; the "NORMALLY" in these documents must be verified against target articles.
- This edition does not pass off external submission conventions as rules from the book. Where journal formatting, supplementary materials or time-sensitive language are involved, check recent articles and author guidelines of the target journal first.
- Exception: [guidelines/14-系统论文结构与体裁.md](guidelines/14-系统论文结构与体裁.md) is an explicitly marked **external** layer distilled from recent systems-conference papers, not from the book. It exists because the book's journal defaults produced the wrong structure for this project's papers; its rules take precedence on structure for systems papers.

## 3. Four Rule Strength Levels

| Marker | Meaning | How to use |
|---|---|---|
| **MUST** | Missing it causes substantive problems in logic, ownership, reproducibility or meaning | Cannot be deleted merely for length; can only be relocated or rephrased |
| **NORMALLY** | Applies to most successful papers, but is affected by readership, discipline and journal | Adopt first, then adjust using target articles |
| **OPTIONAL STRATEGY** | Used to enhance readability, persuasiveness, rhythm or impact | Use only when it serves the main line |
| **CONDITIONAL** | Applies only to specific research types, structures or target readers | Must state the trigger condition |

## 4. Order of Use

### Mandatory principle review before every task

Before drafting, checking, revising or evaluating any paper text, first revisit the relevant principle documents in `guidelines/`, together with the applicable checklist and diagnostic cases. This is a mandatory working step, not a one-time reading requirement. The review may be scoped to the current task, but the agent must reconnect its current decision to the relevant rule strength, conditions, exceptions, completion criteria and examples before acting.

If the task changes from whole-paper structure to a section, paragraph, sentence, language, figure, citation or experimental-method issue, revisit the newly relevant principle before continuing. Do not rely only on memory of an earlier reading or on general writing habits.

### Before drafting

1. Select at least four recent, reasonably short, successfully-published papers from your target journal or similar journals.
2. First look at titles, abstracts, graphics, section headings, the first sentence of each paragraph and the conclusion to build a map of the article.
3. List the achievement, contribution and impact of your study, and rank them by importance.
4. Map section and paragraph functions before writing complete sentences.

### During drafting

1. Open the relevant section guidelines; apply the function sequence first, do not start by hunting for elegant sentences.
2. Write an entry sentence for each paragraph, stating its topic, action or relation to the previous paragraph.
3. Label the role of each sentence: background, claim, evidence, explanation, comparison, limitation, transition, etc.
4. Check that given information is at the start of the sentence and that new information naturally becomes the starting point of the next sentence.
5. Then select tense, voice, modal verbs, connectors and terminology.

### When revising

Work in the following order; do not start by correcting grammar word by word:

1. Whole-paper purpose, readership and take-home message;
2. Transitions between sections and closure of the research question;
3. Single function and internal order of each paragraph;
4. Inter-sentence connection, evidence adjacency and claim strength;
5. Terminology, tense, active/passive, prepositions, articles and sentence length;
6. Graphics, citations, title, abstract, formatting and target journal requirements.

## 6. Terminology Conventions

- **narrative wrap**: the communication scaffold that wraps information, marks function and guides the reader.
- **reverse engineering**: reverse-engineering reading; not only reading content, but also identifying how sentences, paragraphs, titles, graphics and tense deliver content.
- **target articles**: the set of articles used to observe the real writing conventions of the current discipline and journal.
- **achievement**: what the study managed to achieve on its own research question.
- **contribution**: what the study adds to existing knowledge, research lines or real-world applications.
- **impact/application**: what the study may change, or how and by whom it may be used.
- **information gap**: gaps between sentences that the reader must cross on their own but that the writer has not bridged.
- **ownership**: whether the reader can tell whether a sentence is the present writer's own work, another researcher's work, standard knowledge or speculation.
- **happy words**: evaluative words that explicitly signal value, advantage, accuracy, reliability or novelty; not exaggeration, but stating the value the writer is entitled to claim.
